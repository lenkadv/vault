# Playbook: create

Use this for a brand-new plan, or a full rewrite of one that no longer holds. The
plan goes through the review queue as a work item, and only lands in
`brain/plan.md` after the owner says yes.

Read `SKILL.md` first. Its law — sourced or placeholdered, proof only from
`brain/proof/`, honest empty states, placeholders survive, one page, the owner
decides — governs everything below.

## Do these first, in this order

1. Read the brain files listed in `SKILL.md`. All of them.
2. Scan the business's `work/` folder for items already in flight.
3. Name the situation this business is in, out loud, in your reply.
4. Name which arc the page will use, and the one-line reason (Step 3).
5. Confirm this is the create path, not maintain: the change touches the plan's
   target, audience, position, channels, refusals, or the offer under it — or
   there is no real plan yet.
6. Want a fact the folder does not hold? Stop and write
   `[PLACEHOLDER: what is missing · why it matters here · what would settle it]`.
   Then carry on. Never estimate, never round, never "for example".
   The token is `[PLACEHOLDER:` exactly. A bare bracket like `[the closing date]`,
   a dash, or a blank space is **not** a placeholder — it reads like a decision the
   owner made, it travels into `brain/plan.md` looking like one, and it fails the
   sourcing law.
7. When the page is drafted, count its words. Over about 500, cut before review.
8. Before `draft -> review`, write out all eleven bar lines with a pass or fail
   against each, quoting the plan's own words for every pass (Step 6). A bar you did
   not write out counts as one you did not run.
9. Open or close your reply with the five lines below. They are as required as the
   bar.

Then work through the steps below. Two finished plans are in `examples/`:
`plan-starting-from-zero.md` and `plan-capacity-constrained.md`. Read them for the
standard, not for the wording — they use two different arcs on purpose.

## The five lines your first reply must carry

The plan goes in a file. These five go in the **reply**, and they are the half that
gets dropped when a run is tired. They open or close your first reply, always, as
five short lines. A reply without them is unfinished, exactly like a bar you did not
write out.

1. **Situation** — which one, in three or four words.
2. **Arc, and why** — which arc, and the reason from this business.
3. **What did not hold up** — the one thing you cross-examined, or "nothing; I
   looked at the goal, the proof, the old dates and the competitor file."
4. **Idea bank** — three counts: how many ideas are in, how many are later, how
   many are not doing. Or "ideas.md is still template."
5. **What is waiting, and where** — the in-flight work items the plan assumes, or
   "nothing waiting in `work/`" — plus the path of the item you just created.

Write them plainly, one line each. They are not a report; they are the receipt for
the four reads in Step 1 and the arc choice in Step 3, which otherwise leave no
trace anywhere the owner can see.

## The example rule (this one is absolute)

Every example business in this skill is called "Example something" — Example Bike
Repair, Example Clay Studio, Example Bookkeeping. That is deliberate. The fake name
sits inside the example sentences, so if one is ever copied into a real plan, the
fake name travels with it and the mistake is visible immediately.

**No sentence from any example in this skill may ever appear in a real draft.** Not
the numbers, not the reasons, not the phrasing with the details swapped. Examples
show you the standard to hit. Every line you write comes from this business's own
files.

## Step 1: Read the folder, then cross-examine it

Load the brain files listed in `SKILL.md`. Then do these four reads, which most
plans skip:

**Read the queue.** Scan the business's `work/` folder for items at `draft`,
`review`, `changes`, or `approved`. A plan that ignores work already in flight
sets the owner up to fight themselves. Name any in-flight item the plan assumes,
or say in one line that you looked and there was nothing waiting.

**Sweep the idea bank.** Give every idea in `brain/ideas.md` one of three statuses:
in, later (and name the trigger that starts it), or not doing (and name why). If
`ideas.md` is empty or still template, say so in one line and move on. Only the
ideas that change a decision go on the plan page itself — the ones you are doing
show up in the rhythm, the ones you are refusing show up in the refusals, and the
rest stay in your message to the owner.

**Cross-examine.** Name at least one thing in the brain that does not hold up, or
say you looked and it all lined up. Look for: a stated goal the work history does
not match, a claim with no proof behind it, a date in the old plan that has passed,
a competitor note older than the last quarter, an offer in `business.md` that
nothing in `work/` has ever sold.

**Check the earlier links before you plan the later ones.** Marketing sits at the
end of a chain, and a plan that works on the last link while an earlier one is
broken just spends the owner's week. Walk the chain in this order and stop at the
first link that does not hold:

1. **The thing being sold.** Is there an offer, at a price, that someone has
   actually bought? (`brain/business.md`, plus anything in `work/` that sold.)
2. **How it is sold.** Do enquiries turn into customers? (Whatever record the
   business keeps — a job log, invoices, the owner's own account.)
3. **Who it is sold to.** Is there a group that keeps buying?
4. **How they find out.** Only this last link is a channel question.

If link 1, 2, or 3 is the broken one, say so in one plain line and say that the
plan is not going to fix it with more posting. The plan can still be written — it
just points at the real link, and says the later ones were left alone on purpose.

**If this is a rewrite,** open by naming what changed in the world that reopened
the plan, in the owner's own terms. Not "version 2" but, in the shape Example Bike
Repair would use: "Example Bike Repair is reopening this plan because the spring ad
round cost more per booking than referrals did, and because you dropped the
six-week service package last week." Offer to log that reason at the top of
`brain/decisions.md`, dated, and write it only on their yes.

## Step 2: Draft first. Never open with a questionnaire

The first substantive thing the owner sees is a draft, or a plain read of their
situation — never a list of intake questions. Correcting a draft is a small ask.
Answering twenty questions is not, and they will not do it.

Questions are earned. Ask at most three, in one round, and only when:

- the brain genuinely does not hold the answer, **and**
- the answer changes what the plan says.

Pair every question with what it changes: "How many hours a week can you actually
give this? It decides whether the plan carries two channels or one." A question a
brain file already answers is a failure. Zero questions is a pass. If the whole
thing turns out to be a much bigger unknown than three questions can close, say so
plainly and stop, rather than interviewing forever.

## Step 3: Name the situation, and let it shape the page

Before you write anything, name which situation this business is in. Say it out
loud in your reply, and say why the plan is shaped the way it is.

| Situation | What it means here |
|---|---|
| Starting from zero | No channel is working yet; nothing to build on |
| One channel already working | Something brings customers today, and it is not maxed out |
| Season or event driven | The year has peaks the business cannot move |
| Launch driven | A dated thing is coming that everything hangs off |
| Capacity constrained | Demand already beats what they can deliver |

The situation narrows the arc of the page. It does not pick it. **Picking the arc is
a decision you make and write down**, like every other decision in this playbook.

### Choose an arc. Never fall into one

Five arcs. More than one fits most situations, which is the point.

1. **The decision list.** Numbered decisions. Each one carries its own reason, its
   doer, and the condition that would reverse it. There is no separate refusals
   section, because a refusal is a decision, and no separate metrics section,
   because each decision names what would reverse it. The outcome sits at the top
   as the thing every decision serves. *It ends inside the decisions: the last one
   is simply the last one.*
2. **The chain.** Two or three steps, each unlocked by the one before it, with the
   unlock condition written into the step. *It ends on the step that is
   deliberately not started yet, and on what would start it.*
3. **The calendar.** Dates lead. Each date carries the work, the doer, and what has
   to be true by then. *It ends on the last date, and says plainly whether anything
   after it has been decided.*
4. **The one-thing page.** The single move fills the top half. *It ends on what is
   being given up to make room for it.*
5. **The standing arc** — outcome, then who it is for, then what we say, then the
   work, then what we watch, then the checkpoint. This is the plainest arc and the
   easiest to reach for, which is exactly why reaching for it by default is a fail.
   Use it only when you can say in one line why this business's situation calls for
   it. *It ends on the checkpoint.*

**The arc governs the whole page, including how it ends.** The closing sequence —
refusals, watched numbers, the checkpoint, what would reopen it — is part of the
arc, not a tail bolted onto whichever arc you picked. Refusals can lead a page,
sit in the middle, or be the decisions themselves. A watched number can be a
section or can live beside the decision it would overturn. A checkpoint can be a
closing block or can sit inside the step it gates. If your last few plans changed
shape at the top and still finished not-doing, then watched numbers, then
checkpoint, then what-reopens-it, the arc changed and the machine underneath kept
running.

**Write the arc you chose and the one-line reason into the item's checks footer**
(Step 7). The reason names something about this business, not something about
plans in general. "Example Clay Studio can only fire 60 pieces a week, so every
line on the page is that number deciding something — a decision list" is a reason.
"A decision list is clear" is not.

Then check yourself: if the last plan you wrote used this arc too, you need a
second, different reason for reaching for it again. If you cannot write that
reason, the arc was defaulted rather than chosen. Pick another one and lay the same
decisions out differently.

If the business is a mix of two situations, name the mix and build the arc it
needs. There is no required heading list, and there must not be one: two plans that
share a heading list mean the skill wrote the plan, not the business.

## Step 4: Make these eight decisions, in this order

The order is fixed because it is a chain of dependencies. You cannot choose a
channel before you know who you are for. Answer each one before you start the
next. How they get arranged on the page is free (Step 3); the order of the
thinking is not.

### (a) The one outcome for this stretch

Default horizon is 90 days. Shorter is fine when the business honestly cannot see
90 days out — say why. Longer is fine when the sales cycle or the bet is longer —
and then the plan still commits to the 90-day slice and names the longer arc in one
line.

A real target names four things: the number, the unit, the date, and where it will
be seen.

- **Bad:** "Example Dance Studio will grow its email list." "Example Dance Studio
  will significantly increase revenue." Neither can be checked, so neither can
  fail.
- **Good:** "Example Dance Studio has 40 paid trial classes booked by 30 September,
  counted in the Example Dance Studio booking system on 1 October."

If the brain cannot ground a number, write
`[PLACEHOLDER: the specific target — a count, a unit, a date, and where it is seen]`
— that exact token, never a bare bracket like `[target]` — and say what the owner
must supply. Describe the shape of the missing number. Never put an example value
inside a placeholder: a number written to show what a target looks like is still a
number nobody sourced, and it gets read as the answer. Never borrow a benchmark from another business, and never
invent a baseline to make a percentage look real.

### (b) Who it is for, narrowed

Name the narrowest group this business can plausibly be the obvious choice for,
and say what is deliberately out. Fear that the group is small is not a reason to
widen it.

**Derive it from the record, not from a brainstorm.** Look at their own customers:
who stuck, who was easy to serve, who paid without a fight, and — hardest and most
useful — why each took the first call, not why they eventually bought. Bucket the
answers; the pattern names the group. Cite the customers or work items the
narrowing came from. If the business has too few customers to see a pattern, say
so and mark the choice a bet with what would confirm it.

**Narrow is the default and needs no defence.** Broad is allowed only when the plan
names a real structural advantage they already have — an existing distribution
channel, a partner or employer network, a genuine word-of-mouth mechanic — or the
owner states out loud that this is a multi-year commitment. No named advantage
means narrow.

### (c) What we say — the position

Four checks, all written down.

**The alternatives are the real ones.** Draw them from `brain/competitors.md` plus
the owner's own account of what comes up in real conversations, and always include
doing it themselves, carrying on the way they are, and doing nothing. A competitor
nobody is actually weighing is not worth positioning against.

**The competitor sentence test.** Take your positioning sentence and read it as if
each named competitor said it. If any of them could say it, it is not a position —
rewrite it. Write the result of this test into the item's checks footer (Step 7),
so the owner can see it was run and against which names.

If `brain/competitors.md` is empty or still template, the test still runs — say
plainly that no competitor is on file, and run the sentence against the buyer's
real alternatives instead: doing it themselves, carrying on exactly as they are,
and doing nothing. If the sentence is still true when the buyer just carries on as
they are, it is not a position either. Then say that filling `competitors.md` would
make this test sharper.

- **Bad:** "Example Bookkeeping is the leading all-in-one bookkeeping partner for
  growing businesses." Every competitor on the Example Bookkeeping list could say
  that same sentence about themselves. It fails on the first name.
- **Good:** "Example Bookkeeping does the books for van-based trades, filing
  straight from photos of receipts, so nothing lives in a shoebox." Both local
  practices in the Example Bookkeeping `competitors.md` require a monthly folder
  drop-off, so neither could say that without changing how they work.

**The chain is traceable.** Write all four links: the alternatives -> what this
business has that they do not -> what that is worth to this buyer -> who cares most
about that. A position with a missing link is a matter of taste, and nobody will
know when it needs to change.

**The position is today's, not the dream.** The plan carries the position they can
win with now. A longer arc, if the owner has one, is one line and clearly labelled
as the arc. An investor-flavoured or aspirational line inside the plan is a fail.

Then name where the new words get said out loud first — a sales call, a discovery
call, a networking conversation, one email — and what would count as it working:
people leaning in rather than glazing over, or more first conversations turning
into real opportunities.

### (d) Two or three focus channels, each with its evidence

**Two or three focus channels. Fewer when the hours or the brain support fewer, said
out loud — and a plan with a single channel labels it a bet and names what would
confirm it.** Never a list of every channel available. Never zero.

A channel qualifies only by passing **at least two** of these four, and the
evidence goes in writing next to it:

1. **The audience is actually there** — from `brain/audience.md`, `brain/research/`,
   or the owner's own account of where their customers came from.
2. **They already have something there** — samples, a following, past work, proof,
   a track record.
3. **They can keep it up** at the hours they have said they have.
4. **It suits the price and the way the thing is sold.** A high-price,
   relationship-sold offer is not served by a channel that only produces anonymous
   clicks.

If no channel passes two of the four, say so, pick the one with the most evidence,
label it a bet, and name what would confirm it. That is the single-channel case, and
it is a pass — but it is never silent.

- **Bad:** "Instagram, because Example Dance Studio is a visual business." An
  adjective, not a test.
- **Good:** "The local parents' group chat: nine of the last twelve Example Dance
  Studio sign-ups came from a post there (Example Dance Studio booking notes), and
  the owner already posts in that chat most weeks. Passes tests 1 and 2."

Write the evidence clause fresh from what this business's own files actually say.
The evidence is a different kind of thing in every business — a job log, an invoice
run, a reply thread, the owner's own account of a conversation — so a clause that
would fit any business is a clause that checked nothing.

**Never pick a channel from a traffic or attribution report alone.** Those reports
systematically hide where people really came from: direct and search visits soak up
word of mouth, group chats and social; searches for the business's own name are
demand something else created; and people misremember and mis-credit how they found
you, honestly and often. A channel justified only by "the analytics says most
traffic comes from X" does not qualify unless a second, behaviour-based source
agrees — which jobs or sales actually came from where.

### (e) The rhythm — what happens, how often, and who does it

Every recurring action names a doer. No action without a name against it.

If you name a GrowOS skill as the doer, check it exists in `.claude/skills/`. If it
does not, write the action in plain words and mark it as not built yet. Never name
a skill, tool, or integration you have not checked.

**Cost the rhythm against the owner's hours, and show the arithmetic.** In the
shape Example Dance Studio would use: "Two Example Dance Studio posts a week at
about 40 minutes each, plus an hour of replies, is about two hours a week against
the four you said you have."

Three capacity situations, and each one produces a **structurally different note**,
not the same note with different words. Pick by what the folder actually holds:

- **Hours stated.** The note is a sum. Add the work up and set it against the number
  they gave you.
- **Hours unknown.** The note is an assumption with a consequence. Name what the
  plan is built on, in the positive, and say what changes if it is wrong: "this
  plan assumes about two hours a week; at one, step 2 drops." **Never open by
  telling the owner what they have not told you.** "You have not told me your
  hours" and "your hours are not written down" are the same move in different
  words, and neither of them says anything about this business.
- **More than one person.** The note is an allocation, not a total: who does what.
  If the plan names work that nobody owns, say that plainly — the unowned line is
  the finding, and the hours are beside the point until it has a name against it.

Never recommend more work than the owner said they have time for without saying it
out loud.

### (f) The refusals

At least one, each with a one-line reason tied to this business's own facts. Zero
refusals is a fail. This is the part that makes a plan read as decided rather than
generated, and it is the part every generated plan leaves out.

- **Bad:** "Example Bike Repair will focus on quality over quantity." Refuses
  nothing.
- **Good:** "Example Bike Repair runs no paid ads this quarter: the last two
  Example Bike Repair rounds cost more per booking than the referral route did, and
  there is nobody free to watch a daily budget."

The ideas you marked "not doing" in Step 1 usually belong here.

**The count comes from the brain, and nothing else.** One refusal is a fine answer.
Five is a fine answer. What is never fine is adding a refusal to round the list up
to a comfortable number, or merging two genuinely different declines into one line
to keep the list tidy. Both are stock shape wearing a refusal's clothes. If your
list has the same number of items as the last plan you wrote, check whether the
brain produced that number or whether you did.

**A channel rule is not a refusal.** "We post twice a week, not daily" is a decision
about rhythm, and it belongs in (e). A refusal is something this business could
plausibly do this horizon, that somebody has actually been tempted by, and that it
is deciding against. If a line on the refusal list would not have happened anyway,
it is padding.

### (g) One or two numbers to watch, and where each is seen

One or two. Not three. Each one says where it is looked at. If it needs a
dashboard to answer, it is too complicated for a one-page plan.

### (h) The checkpoint, and what reopens the plan

Name one checkpoint date and what happens at it. Any step that depends on an
earlier one says so in plain words — the shape Example Dance Studio would use is
"only if Example Dance Studio has its first twenty sign-ups by 15 August". A plan
written this way can still be honest on day 60, because it said in advance what
would make it stop.

**Choose the checkpoint's logic, not just its wording.** Rewriting the same
underlying move in fresh words is not variety — it is the same machine with a new
paint job. Four logics, and they are genuinely different things:

1. **A question the date must answer.** The checkpoint exists because something is
   unknown, and by that date it will be known. The plan says what the answer
   decides.
2. **A stop rule.** If X has not happened by the date, stop doing Y — or do not
   start Z. Nothing moves anywhere else.
3. **A reallocation.** The hours or the money in one named line move to another
   named line.
4. **A confirmation.** The likeliest outcome is that nothing changed. The plan says
   what "nothing changed" looks like, and that it carries on unaltered.

Pick from the situation. A business that does not yet know something needs the
question. A business pouring effort into an unproven thing needs the stop rule. A
business with a fixed budget of hours needs the reallocation. A business whose plan
is broadly working needs the confirmation, and saying so out loud is a real answer,
not a weak one.

**Never reach for the same logic twice running.** If the last plan you wrote ended
on a reallocation and this one is heading the same way, write down why this
business's situation calls for it again. If you cannot, you reached for the engine
rather than choosing it. One shape in particular has a habit of coming back: a
condition, then hours moved from one thing to another, then a colon, then a
correction aimed at whatever the business posts. Once you have noticed yourself
writing it, it is stock.

The same applies to the line that says what would reopen the plan: name the actual
condition in this business's terms, not a stock phrase about the plan reopening.

## Step 5: Write it as one page

Cutting is the craft. Every line the owner will not act on gets cut on sight:
mission statements, market-size paragraphs, persona stories, strengths-and-weaknesses
grids, funnel diagrams, maturity scores, and context paragraphs that set up
something the owner already knows.

**Count the words of the plan page.** Not an estimate — count them. Over about 500,
cut until it is under, and cut before the plan goes to review, never after. Say in
your reply what you cut to get there.

### Write the joins fresh, every time

The lines that carry a plan from one decision to the next are where sameness
creeps in, because they feel like plumbing rather than thinking. They are not
plumbing. Four in particular must be built from this business's own facts every
time, and never reused from a plan you wrote before:

- **The checkpoint line** — and its underlying logic, not only its wording (4h).
- **The line that says what would reopen the plan** — the real condition, named.
- **The line about the owner's hours** — which must never be a negated disclosure
  ("you have not told me your hours…") followed by a budget worked out from their
  existing routine. That frame is banned outright, in any wording.
- **The evidence clause next to each channel** — the actual record it rests on.
- **The closing sequence itself** — the order the page ends in, which belongs to
  the arc (Step 3).

Test it like this: if a sentence would sit just as comfortably in a plan for a
completely different business, it is stock copy and it is telling the owner
nothing. Rewrite it from their files.

Then run **the swap test**: read the plan with the business's name replaced by a
named competitor's. If every line stays equally true, the plan is generic and
fails. Name three lines that would become untrue. If the brain is thin enough that
fewer than three lines are specific to this business, say that plainly and name
what would fill it — that is an honest pass, not a fudge.

With no competitor on file, swap in the buyer's real alternatives instead: read the
plan as if it belonged to someone doing this for themselves, or to the business the
buyer would carry on with if nothing changed. The question is the same — which
lines stop being true? Say that no competitor was on file when you ran it.

Write it in the owner's plain words. This page is internal, so it is grade-8 plain
English, not marketing copy.

## Step 6: The pass bar — run it before the owner sees anything

All eleven must pass. Each one has an honest route for a business whose folder is
still thin, so none of them ever needs to be faked.

1. It fits one page (about 500 words or fewer), and every line is something the
   owner can act on or answer yes or no to.
2. It names one outcome for the stretch with a number, a unit, a date, and where it
   will be seen — **or** a `[PLACEHOLDER: ...]` saying exactly what the owner must
   supply.
3. It names who the plan is for, narrower than "our customers", and says where that
   came from — **or** says in one line that the brain cannot support a narrowing
   yet, and names what would settle it.
4. Two or three focus channels, each with a one-line why citing its evidence.
   **Or** fewer, when the hours or the brain support fewer and the plan says so out
   loud — and where that leaves a single channel, it is labelled a bet with what
   would confirm it.
5. At least one refusal, each with a one-line why.
6. Every recurring action names a doer. Any named skill exists in
   `.claude/skills/`, or is written in plain words and marked not built yet.
7. One or two watched numbers, each saying where it is seen. Not three.
8. Any step that depends on an earlier one says so, and a checkpoint date is named.
9. The weekly arithmetic does not exceed the owner's stated hours — **or**, if the
   hours are unknown, the plan says so and states the assumption it used.
10. Every number, price, claim and competitor reference is sourced or is a
    `[PLACEHOLDER: ...]` — that exact token, not a bare bracket, a dash, or a
    blank. No exceptions.
11. You can name which situation shaped this plan, and name three lines that would
    become untrue if the business name were swapped for a competitor's — **or**,
    when the brain is too thin for three, say so plainly and name what would fill it.

**Write the bar out, and quote the page for every pass.** Before you move the item
to `review`, list all eleven lines in the item's checks footer (Step 7) with a plain
pass or fail against each.

**Every pass carries the exact words from the plan that satisfy it, in quotes.** Not
a description of how it passed — the words themselves, copied from the page. If you
cannot find the words to quote, the line does not pass, however sure you feel. That
is the whole rule, and it is the one that stops a bar becoming a row of ticks.

Where a line offers two routes, the written line **names the route and then quotes
the page text taking it**. "Line 4: pass, bet route — 'At five customers that is a
bet; step 1 confirms it.'" is a pass. "Line 4: pass, evidence is strong" is not.

A bar you ran in your head and did not write out counts as a bar you did not run. A
failed line is not a reason to hide the bar; it is a reason to fix the line, or to
say plainly in your reply what still fails and why.

This bar is the plan's gate. There is no separate editor pass on the plan, because
the plan is an internal working document written in plain words, not copy the owner
will put in front of customers. Do not skip the bar because the plan "reads fine".

## Step 7: The work item

One markdown file:

```
<business>/work/strategy/<readable-slug>.md
```

Frontmatter you set:

```yaml
---
type: marketing-plan
headline: "The 90-day plan, drafted from the brain"
skill: "marketing-strategy"
---
```

The system stamps `id`, `status: draft`, `business`, `channel`, `created`, and the
empty `note`. Let the item be born `draft`. Never create it already `approved` or
`published`; the guard denies it, and it would skip the owner's yes.

**The item body is the proposed plan itself**, written in the exact shape
`brain/plan.md` will take — not a memo about the plan.

Then, below the plan and clearly separated, the item carries one footer:

```markdown
## Checks (not part of the plan page)
```

Everything under that heading is working-out for the owner, and none of it is ever
copied into `brain/plan.md`. It holds three things:

- **The arc**, and the one-line reason you chose it (Step 3).
- **The competitor sentence test**: the sentence, the names you ran it against, and
  the result. If no competitor is on file, say so and name the alternatives you ran
  it against instead.
- **The eleven bar lines**, each with a pass or fail and a short phrase on how it
  passed (Step 6).

The idea-bank sweep, what you cross-examined, and any open questions go in your
chat message rather than the item, so the file stays about the plan — as the five
required reply lines at the top of this playbook.

When every bar line passes, move `draft -> review`, one save, and tell the owner in
one short line that the plan is waiting for them.

## Step 8: On approval, write the brain file and complete the receipt

Only after the item is at `approved` — the owner's own move, or applied on their
word by the `review-queue` skill. **First check the item's own `type:` field.
Proceed only when it reads exactly `marketing-plan`.** Any other type that has
landed in `work/strategy/` — `campaign-brief`, `seo-report`,
`marketing-report`, `website-audit`, `offer`, or anything else — is not the
plan. Stop here, write nothing to `brain/plan.md`, and tell the owner plainly
that this item is not the plan, and name what it actually is.

1. **Write the plan into `brain/plan.md`.** Copy the item body down to the
   "Checks" heading and no further — the checks footer never goes into the brain.
   Replace the plan content — except the `## Social` section: if the current
   `brain/plan.md` already has one, carry it over byte-for-byte into the new
   plan (same position, or the end if the new plan has no matching spot), and
   say why in one sentence — `social-strategy` owns that section, not this
   skill. Do not keep stale lines from the old plan because they
   look harmless; a line that is no longer true is worse than no line. Carry every
   `[PLACEHOLDER: ...]` across exactly as written.
2. **Read the file back** and compare it to the approved item body above the
   "Checks" heading. They must match.
3. **Complete the receipt on the work item**, then move `approved -> published` in
   the same save:

```yaml
publish_destination: brain/plan.md
publish_ref: <business>/brain/plan.md
publish_attempted_at: 2026-07-30T12:00:00Z
published_at: 2026-07-30T12:00:04Z
publish_state: prepared
publish_note: "Plan written to brain/plan.md and read back to verify."
status: published
```

Both times are exact UTC in the form `YYYY-MM-DDTHH:MM:SSZ`. Set
`publish_state: prepared` only after the read-back matched. `publish_note` holds a
plain outcome line and never a secret or the owner's creative feedback.

4. **Tell the owner in one line** where the plan now lives — and say it only from
   the read-back you just did in step 2. The sentence describes the file as you
   have this moment seen it, never the write you intended. If step 2 did not match,
   you are in step 9, not here.

## Step 9: If the write fails, say so

If `brain/plan.md` cannot be written, or the read-back does not match:

- Leave the item at `approved`. Do not move it to `published`.
- Set `publish_state: blocked` and put the reason in `publish_note` in plain words
  ("could not write brain/plan.md: the file is read-only").
- Tell the owner what did not happen and what you would do next.

Never claim the plan landed when it did not. A blocked state is a normal, honest
state; a false receipt is not.

## Banned in a plan

Any one of these is a defect, not a matter of taste.

- A fixed set of headings reproduced whatever the business is; a default
  month-one, month-two, month-three arc.
- An arc that was fallen into rather than chosen: no named arc and no written
  reason in the checks footer, or the same arc as the last plan with the same
  reason behind it.
- **Recurring stock sentences.** A checkpoint line, a what-would-reopen-it line, an
  owner's-hours line, or a channel-evidence clause that could be lifted whole into
  another business's plan and still read correctly. These four are where sameness
  hides, and a sentence that fits any business proves nothing about this one.
- **The negated-disclosure hours frame**, in any wording: opening with what the
  owner has not told you and then deriving a budget from their existing routine.
  Say what the plan assumes, not what the owner failed to say.
- **A recurring checkpoint engine** — the same inner logic reached for plan after
  plan, however differently it is worded. In particular: a condition, then hours
  moved from one line to another, then a colon, then a correction aimed at whatever
  the business posts.
- **A fixed closing sequence** — every plan finishing not-doing, then watched
  numbers, then checkpoint, then what-reopens-it, whatever arc it opened with. The
  ending belongs to the arc.
- **A refusal count that came from the list rather than from the brain** — a
  decline added to round the number up, or two different declines merged to keep it
  tidy. And a channel or rhythm rule dressed up as a refusal.
- A sentence taken from an example in this skill, with or without the details
  swapped.
- A target with no number, or a number with no source.
- More than three focus channels, or any channel with neither an evidence line nor
  a bet label naming what would confirm it.
- A channel chosen because a traffic or attribution report credited it.
- An action with no doer; a skill named that does not exist in `.claude/skills/`.
- More than two watched numbers.
- Any line the owner cannot act on or answer yes or no to: mission statements,
  strengths-and-weaknesses grids, market-size paragraphs, persona stories, funnel
  diagrams, maturity scores.
- A benchmark from another business presented as a fact about this one.
- A score or grade "assessed from the materials" — a confident number resting on
  nothing.
- A bar line marked passed with no quotable words behind it, or marked passed on a
  route the page does not actually take.
- A bare bracket, a dash, or a blank standing in for a missing fact. The token is
  `[PLACEHOLDER:` or the gap is not declared at all.
- Quietly overwriting `brain/plan.md`, or rewriting history in
  `brain/decisions.md`.
- Recommending more work than the owner's stated hours without saying so.
- Naming a method after a framework, or leaning on a famous named model to carry
  the thinking. The decision rules above stand on their own.

## What this playbook never does

It never writes `brain/plan.md` before the owner approves the item. It never
invents a target, a baseline, a customer result, or a competitor fact. It never
fills or deletes a placeholder. It never creates an item already approved or
published, and never jumps `review` to `published`. It never writes the `note`
field. It never ships a plan that failed the bar in Step 6 without saying, plainly,
which line failed and why, and it never moves a plan to `review` without writing the
bar out with the plan's own words quoted against every pass. It never marks a bar
line passed on a route the page does not take. It never leaves a bare bracket
standing in for a fact. It never copies a sentence from an example in this skill
into a real plan.
