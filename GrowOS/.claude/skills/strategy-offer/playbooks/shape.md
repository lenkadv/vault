# Playbook: shape a new offer

Use this when there is nothing written down yet: a new thing to sell, a first offer
for a new business, or an offer that exists only in the owner's head. Run it inside
the method in `SKILL.md`, which holds the law every mode obeys.

Every business, name and number in this file is made up to show the shape of a
good answer. None of it is a fact about anyone, and none of it may be copied into
real work.

## Step 1: read the record before you ask the owner anything

Open these, in this order. Each one answers a different question.

| File | What you are looking for |
|---|---|
| `brain/business.md` | What they sell now, the prices, the ladder, the hard facts |
| `brain/audience.md` | Who this is for in their own words, the objections, who is explicitly not the customer |
| `brain/methodology.md` | How they actually do the work — the source of a real differentiator |
| `brain/competitors.md` | What the alternatives promise, and when that was recorded |
| `brain/proof/` | What can honestly be claimed, and whether each entry is approved for use |
| `brain/compliance.md` | What may and may not be said, and who signs off |
| `brain/voice.md` | How the shippable wording has to sound |
| `brain/decisions.md` | Offers already tried, dropped, or ruled out, and why |
| `brain/ideas.md` | Things the owner already wanted to sell |
| `work/` | Anything about this offer already in flight, so you build on it instead of clashing |

**Cross-examine what you read; do not transcribe it.** Name at least one of these
out loud, or say plainly that you looked and found none:

- a goal stated in the brain that the work history does not match,
- a claim in `business.md` with nothing in `proof/` behind it,
- a price or a competitor note old enough that it may no longer be true,
- a decision in `decisions.md` that cuts against what is being proposed now.

## Step 2: draft first

Write a first version of the whole offer from what the record already gives you,
straight into the draft work item. Do not open with questions. Something real has
to exist before anyone is asked anything, because a draft the owner corrects in two
minutes beats an interview they abandon.

**The owner does not read the document yet.** It sits at `draft` until the Editor
gate has run, and they see it at `review`. What they get from you in the meantime
is a short read of their situation in plain words — what you found in the record,
what you have drafted from it, and what is missing — plus any question that earns
its place. Draft-first is about the order of work, not about handing over a
pre-gate document.

Where the record genuinely does not reach, write `[PLACEHOLDER: what is missing]`
right there in the draft: what is missing, why it matters here, and what would
settle it. A gap the owner fills beats a guess that reads as fact.

**The moment you want a number or a benchmark the folder does not hold: stop.** It
will not feel like inventing. It will feel like being useful. And naming the gap
does not buy you the right to fill it — the failure is saying "I do not have this"
and then supplying it from memory in the next sentence.

At that moment, write this and nothing else:

`[PLACEHOLDER: what is missing · why it matters here · what would settle it]`

**Never bridge the gap from memory.** "I do not have this" is always followed by the
placeholder, never by what businesses like this one usually do, usually charge, or
usually include. You do not know that about this business, and a remembered figure
sitting next to their real ones reads as research. This covers example figures,
brackets and ranges as well — a number does not become safe by being an
illustration. Every money figure in your reply traces to the folder, to the owner
this session, or to arithmetic whose inputs you have shown, or it does not appear.

**BAD.** "Your folder does not say how many sessions people usually buy. In this
kind of coaching, six to eight is the standard block."

**GOOD.** "Your folder does not say how many sessions people usually buy.
`[PLACEHOLDER: how many sessions a client typically books · it decides whether this
is sold as a block or one at a time · a look back at your last ten clients.]`"

## Step 3: the questions you are allowed to ask

After the draft, not before. One round, three at most, and every one paired in your
own head with "because it changes X". A question the brain already answers is a
mistake. Zero questions is a pass.

The three that most often earn their place when shaping a first offer:

- "What do you already do on every job that you have never charged for or written
  down?" — because it is where a real differentiating component comes from.
- "Who have you turned away, or wished you had?" — because it writes the not-for
  line, and the not-for line is what makes the right people lean in.
- "Is there anything in here you would not want to promise every time?" — because
  it stops you writing an obligation the owner never agreed to.

## Step 4: build the offer, in this order

The order matters. You cannot pick what goes in the box before you know who the box
is for, and you cannot price it before you know what it is.

### a. The promise: buyer, outcome, boundary

A stranger who reads only the promise should be able to say who it is for and what
they get. Three parts, all present:

- **Buyer** — the specific person, not "businesses" or "customers".
- **Outcome** — what is different for them afterwards, in their words. Use the
  customer's own phrasing where `proof/`, `inbox/` or `audience.md` supplies it.
  Never invent a quote to get a nicer sentence.
- **Boundary** — where it stops. What is not included, or who it is not for, or
  what it does not fix. The boundary is what makes the promise believable.

**GOOD — a children's swim school.** "Ten Saturday mornings that take a four- to
six-year-old from holding the wall to swimming a width on their own. Eight children
to a teacher, the same teacher every week. If they can already swim a width, this
is the wrong class."

**BAD, same business.** "Fun, confidence-building swimming lessons in a safe and
supportive environment." No buyer, no outcome anyone could check, no edge.

**GOOD — a firm that inspects warehouse racking.** "A written racking report a
warehouse manager can hand straight to their insurer, from one visit, covering
every bay on a site up to ten thousand pallet positions. We do not repair what we
find. We tell you what has to come off the racks today and what can wait."

**BAD, same business.** "Comprehensive health and safety solutions for the modern
warehouse."

**GOOD — a mobile welder who fixes farm machinery on the farm.** "We come to the
broken machine instead of you hauling it in. Same day inside thirty miles, a price
agreed before we start, and we say no to structural work on road-going trailers —
that needs a certified shop."

**BAD, same business.** "Reliable, high-quality mobile welding with unmatched
service."

### b. Who it is for, and who it is not for

Both. Each with a one-line reason. An offer for everyone converts nobody, and the
exclusion is what makes the right buyer feel it was built for them.

Draw the not-for line from `brain/audience.md` (it usually already names who is not
the customer), from `decisions.md`, or from the owner's answer in Step 3. Write it
as a kindness, not a snub: who this would waste money on, and where they would be
better served.

### c. What is included, and the job each piece does

Every element names the obstacle it removes. Anything you cannot write a job for
comes out.

- **No count quotas, ever.** There is no right number of extras. Zero extras is a
  correct, passing answer when that is the truth.
- **Only what already exists.** Include only what the business already does, or
  what the owner has explicitly agreed in this session to build. Writing a
  deliverable into an offer creates an obligation the owner may not have noticed
  they took on.
- **Never attach a money value to an included item.** "Worth $400" is a number
  nobody measured.

The strongest included pieces are usually things the business already does for free
and has never written down: the pre-visit phone call, the spare parts carried as
standard, the photos taken before the work starts.

### d. At least one differentiating component

A component is a thing the buyer can weigh and decide on. An adjective is not.
Without components, price is the only variable left, and the cheapest quote wins.

Build the list from `methodology.md` and `competitors.md`: what does this business
actually do that the alternatives do not reliably do? State each as a decision the
buyer can make.

**GOOD.** "Every fitter carries the same spare parts, so a callback is a same-day
fix instead of a re-order." · "The quote is capped: if the job runs long, that is
ours to absorb." · "Your report is written by the person who did the visit, not by
someone reading their notes."

**BAD.** "Premium quality." · "Best-in-class support." · "Unmatched attention to
detail." Superlatives never count as components.

**The honest empty state.** If the business genuinely has no component the
alternatives lack, say exactly that, and name what would create one: "Nothing in
`methodology.md` names something the three recorded competitors do not also do. The
nearest candidate is the pre-visit call, which they may do too — worth asking a
customer who switched." Never manufacture a difference to fill this section.

### e. The guarantee, resolved one of three ways

First, open `brain/compliance.md`. If it is missing or still template, say so once
in the document and mark the guarantee as needing the owner's confirmation. Never
draft a results promise for a regulated trade without looking.

A guarantee must state three things:

1. **Trigger** — exactly what has to happen for it to fire.
2. **Remedy** — exactly what the buyer gets.
3. **Cost if it fires** — what the business loses when it does.

The section then resolves one of exactly three ways, and all three pass:

- **A guarantee with all three parts.** "Say stop before day thirty and you keep
  the plan we wrote and pay nothing further. Trigger: you tell us to stop inside
  thirty days. Remedy: no further invoices, the written plan is yours. Cost to us
  if it fires: the first month of delivery, unrecovered."
- **No guarantee, with the reason and the real assurance.** "We do not offer money
  back — once the crew has worked the day, the hours are spent. What we do stand
  behind: anything we damage is repaired or replaced under our insurance, and the
  capped price holds even if the job runs long. If we ever move to fixed-scope
  jobs, a money-back promise becomes possible. It is not today."
- **A placeholder naming the decision the owner has to make.**
  `[PLACEHOLDER: whether a refund is offered inside the first month, and what it
  costs us if it fires — nothing in the folder says.]`

**BAD.** "100% satisfaction guaranteed or your money back!" on a business that
sells labour and cannot refund spent hours. No trigger, no remedy, no cost check,
and an obligation the owner never agreed to.

A guarantee so easy to clear that it would never fire is not a guarantee. One with
no ceiling on what it could cost is not honourable. Aim for a clear trigger and a
bounded cost.

### f. Risk reversal that answers the real hesitation

Find the actual hesitation in `brain/audience.md` objections or `brain/inbox/`, and
dissolve that one — not a generic one. The buyer must be able to see the risk move.

If the recorded objection is "I cannot tell whether it will suit us until we have
tried it", then a trial, a sample, a first small paid piece of work, or a
pre-visit assessment answers it. A longer warranty does not; it answers a different
fear. If nothing in the brain records a hesitation, say so and name the question
that would surface one.

### g. Urgency: real, or honestly absent

Either a genuine reason to act now that traces to the brain or to the owner's own
words this session — a season, a capacity limit that really exists, a cost that
really rises — or a plain statement that there is not one and what would create a
real one later.

Fake countdowns, "only three spots left" with no roster behind it, and "closes
Friday" with no reason are banned outright. The honest absence is a passing answer
and reads as more trustworthy than a manufactured deadline.

### h. Gate every claim against `brain/proof/`

Before the document reaches the owner, mark every claim in it one of three ways:

- **Supportable** — a named entry in `proof/` backs it, and that entry is approved
  for use.
- **Not supportable** — nothing backs it. Soften it to what is true, or cut it.
- **Supportable if the owner supplies X** — name exactly what would make it
  claimable.

If `proof/` is empty, say that once and mark every claim accordingly. That is a
pass, not a failure. Quote any proof you do use word for word, and only if its
recorded approval allows it. A kind email is not permission.

### i. Packaging: pick the shape by a stated rule

| Shape | When it fits |
|---|---|
| One thing for everyone | Few genuinely different buyers, few genuinely different problems |
| A core plus add-ons | One buyer, one main problem, real variation in what people need bolted on |
| Two or three tiers | The same buyer wanting more of the same value as they grow |
| Separate packages | Genuinely different buyers with genuinely different jobs |
| Pay per unit | The customer's need varies a lot each time and the unit tracks value |

The rule: how many genuinely different buyers there are, multiplied by how many
genuinely different problems the business solves. Few of both means simple. Many of
both means more flexible. **Different buyers with different jobs get separate
packages, not tiers** — tiers only make sense when demand really graduates.

Write the rule and the answer into the document, in one line, so the owner can
argue with the reasoning and not just the outcome.

**The tier test.** For each tier, write one sentence naming who it is for and what
problem it solves. If you cannot write that sentence, the tiers came from a feature
list, not from jobs — and the predictable result is a buyer who needs two things
from the top tier being pushed up there and then discounted back down.

**A useful default for a small business with one real offer:** two tiers, where the
higher one is what you present first and the lower one appears only when the buyer
raises budget. The two tiers must differ in components the business already has.

### j. The wording, the name, and where it gets said first

- **Shippable wording.** Write the promise line, the guarantee wording and the
  price presentation as the owner would put them on a page, in the voice from
  `brain/voice.md`. Missing facts stay as placeholders.
- **The name.** Two or three options, plain over clever, and recommend the
  plainest specific one. Or keep the existing name and give a one-line reason.
- **Where it gets tested.** Name where this wording gets said out loud before it
  goes on a page — a discovery call, a quote conversation, one email, a
  conversation at a trade counter — and what counts as it working: people leaning
  in and asking a follow-up question, or a better rate of first conversations that
  turn into real opportunities.
- **When each piece retires.** Anything included to remove an obstacle names the
  condition under which it is no longer needed. An offer element is load-bearing
  for a stage, not forever.

## Step 5: cost it against what the owner can actually do

Check the offer against the owner's stated hours, money and existing assets, and
show the arithmetic. If delivering it at any real volume would take more hours than
they have, say so with the numbers. If their hours are unknown, say so and state
the assumption you used.

Recommending something the owner cannot deliver is not ambition; it is a plan to
break a promise to a customer.

## Step 6: the generic test

Swap the business's name for a named competitor from `brain/competitors.md`. If
every line stays equally true, the offer is generic and fails. Name three lines
that would become untrue.

If `competitors.md` is empty or still template, do not invent a rival to swap in.
Run the same test against the buyer's real alternatives instead — doing it
themselves, carrying on the way they are now, doing nothing at all — and say
plainly that no competitor is on file. Those three are always real, and the status
quo is the alternative most offers actually lose to.

If the brain is thin enough that fewer than three lines are specific to this
business, say so plainly and name what would fill it — a customer conversation, a
look at what they actually do on a job, filling in `methodology.md`.

## Step 7: write the document

One file, `work/strategy/<slug>.md`, `type: offer`, born a draft, as `SKILL.md`
specifies. Order the document around whatever is doing the work in this round, not
around this playbook's step list. If `proof/` is empty and half the claims are
unsupportable, the gaps belong near the top, not in an appendix at the bottom.

## After the owner approves

The brain sync is in `SKILL.md`, and one part of it is easy to get wrong: **when
the owner's approval already says yes to changing `brain/business.md`, do not ask a
second time.** Show the exact before and after **and** apply it in the same turn,
then read it back and complete the receipt. Stopping at a preview after an owner
has plainly said "put it in the business file" leaves the brain unwritten while
everyone believes the offer landed. The second confirmation exists only for an
approval that never mentioned the brain file.

## Banned in an offer

Any one of these is a defect, not a style choice.

- **Any quota that can only be met by inventing.** "Two to four bonuses." "Each
  with a perceived value." "At least three testimonials." A count that forces
  invention is a fabrication instruction wearing an output spec.
- A money value written next to an included item.
- Fake scarcity: countdowns, "only three spots left", "closing Friday" with no real
  reason recorded anywhere.
- Invented social proof of any kind — testimonials, customer names, results, star
  counts, logo walls, "trusted by N businesses" — or real proof used outside what
  its recorded approval allows.
- Outcome or return claims nothing supports: "clients typically triple their
  revenue", "most customers save 20 hours a week".
- Industry benchmarks stated as facts about this business.
- A guarantee the business has not agreed to honour, or one written without opening
  `compliance.md`.
- A deliverable the business does not have and has not agreed in this session to
  build.
- "Who it is for" with no "who it is not for".
- Superlatives standing in for a component the buyer could actually choose.
- Category-creation language with nothing behind it: "we are creating the category
  of...", "the leading platform for...".
- Naming a skill, tool or integration without checking it exists.
- **Any sentence lifted from `examples/`.** Those are written for invented
  businesses and every one of them names the business it belongs to. A real offer
  that contains one is a copy, not a draft.

## The pass bar, before the Editor gate

Every line must pass. Each one has an honest route for a business whose folder is
nearly empty, so a clean, truthful answer can always satisfy it.

**Write the bar out line by line, with a pass or a fail against each line**, in the
work item or in your reply, before the Editor gate. A bar you ran in your head did
not run. "All twelve pass" is not the bar either — show which line said what.

1. The promise names buyer, outcome and boundary, and a stranger could restate who
   it is for and what they get.
2. Who it is for **and** who it is not for are both there, each with a reason —
   **or** the not-for line stands as a `[PLACEHOLDER: who this is not for]` naming
   the exclusion the owner must confirm.
3. Every included piece names the job it does. Nothing sits there without one. Zero
   extras passes if that is the truth.
4. At least one differentiating component is stated as a decision the buyer can
   make, traced to a named source — **or** the document says plainly that the
   business has none yet and names what would create one.
5. The guarantee is resolved one of the three ways: trigger, remedy and cost if it
   fires · "no guarantee" with a reason · a placeholder naming the decision. It was
   checked against `compliance.md`, and if that file is missing or template the
   document says so once.
6. Urgency is resolved honestly: a real reason to act now that traces to a source,
   **or** a plain statement that there is not one and what would create one.
7. Every claim is marked supportable, not supportable, or supportable if the owner
   supplies X. An empty `proof/` is stated once and every claim marked accordingly
   — that is a pass.
8. Any proof used is word for word and inside what its recorded approval allows.
9. The price presentation is shippable wording, and any comparison used to make the
   price make sense is a true one. No struck-through price that was never charged,
   no invented per-item value — **or** the price stands as a `[PLACEHOLDER: ...]`
   naming the number the owner must supply, with the wording around it shippable
   the moment it lands.
10. Shippable wording is present for the promise line, the guarantee and the price
    — **or** the price stands as a `[PLACEHOLDER: ...]` naming the number the owner
    must supply, with the wording around it shippable the moment it lands.
11. Two or three name options with the plainest specific one recommended — **or**
    the existing name kept with a one-line reason.
12. Nothing in the document obliges the business to deliver anything it does not
    already have or has not agreed in this session to build.

Fix anything that fails before you invoke the reviewer. The gate reads for voice
and machine tells; this bar is yours.
