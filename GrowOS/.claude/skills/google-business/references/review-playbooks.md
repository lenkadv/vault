# Review playbooks

## How to pick

Every real Google review gets read once, in full, before deciding which
playbook applies. Pick by what the review actually says, not by its star
count alone — a 4-star review with a real complaint buried in it needs
the negative playbook's caution on the complaint, and a 2-star review
that is mostly a misunderstanding resolved by the end still deserves
warmth where it is warranted.

- **Positive** — nothing to defend, nothing to fix. Below.
- **Negative** — a real complaint, problem, or disappointment. Below.
- **Mixed** — real praise and a real complaint in the same review. Open
  with the positive playbook's warmth for the part that earned it, then
  apply every negative-playbook rule to the part that did not. Never let
  the warm opening soften or excuse the complaint — the two run side by
  side, not blended into one mushy paragraph.

A rating with no written text: a high rating gets a short, genuine thanks
(the positive playbook, compressed). A low rating with nothing written
gets a short, calm, generic reply — there is nothing specific to respond
to, so do not invent a guess at what might be wrong.

## The positive playbook

**The one job:** make a happy customer feel seen, in a way that reads
true to a stranger scrolling past it two years from now.

**Thank specifically.** Reference something the review actually said —
the job that got done, the person who helped, the detail they bothered to
mention. "Thanks for the kind words" could sit under any review on the
internet; a line that could only sit under THIS review is the real thing.

**Voice-true.** This is the business talking, in public, in its own name
— `brain/voice.md` applies here exactly as it does to anything else the
business publishes.

**Vary the opening — every time.** Google shows a business's replies as a
running list under its profile. A column of replies that all start
"Thank you so much for..." reads as templated the moment a visitor
scrolls past three of them, and that costs more than any individual reply
gains. Across a batch, rotate real openings:

- Echo the specific thing they praised, first ("The new patio setup — so
  glad that landed for you.").
- Open with their name, if given ("Maria, this made our week.").
- Open with a short reaction before the thanks ("This is exactly the kind
  of visit we hope for.").
- Ask nothing — just state the specific thing, warmly, and stop.

Never open two replies in the same batch the same way. If a batch runs
long enough that the patterns start repeating, that repetition is worth
noticing and breaking on purpose, not something to let ride.

**Keep it short.** A sentence or two is normal. A five-sentence reply to
a one-line review reads as trying too hard.

**No pitch.** A reply is not the place for an upsell, a "and don't forget
to book your next appointment," or a mention of a different offer —
unless the review itself was already about booking something next, in
which case one natural, low-pressure line is fine. When in doubt, leave
it out; the thanks is the whole job.

## The negative playbook

**Which contact channel counts as "real" for the offline move.** Prefer a
channel filed as customer-facing: a support or contact email, the business
phone, the address on file. An address that exists but is filed for a
different job — a marketing sending address in `setup.md`'s email-channel
entry, a personal inbox — is NOT the default: routing complaints there
buries them in a tool nobody watches for support. Nothing customer-facing
on file: `[PLACEHOLDER: a real way to reach the business directly]`, and
tell the owner which addresses DO exist and what they are filed for, so
the pick is theirs.

**The one goal, stated plainly: this reply is for the future reader, not
the reviewer.** Almost nobody changes their mind because of a public
comment box, and trying to win the argument in front of everyone is how a
bad review turns into a worse thread. The actual audience is the next
hundred people who read this profile before they decide whether to call.
What they are looking for is proof the business handles a problem like an
adult — calm, brief, and human. Write for them.

**Acknowledge without arguing.** Say plainly that this was not the
experience the business wants someone to have. Do not relitigate the
review point by point, do not explain why the reviewer is wrong, and do
not stack up context that reads as excuse-making. One honest, short
acknowledgment beats a paragraph of defense every time.

**Never invent a fact about the case.** No guessing at what an employee
did, why something ran late, or what actually went wrong. The owner has
not told you the specifics: the reply does not contain any — it
acknowledges the reviewer's experience as they describe it, without
confirming or adding a single new detail about what occurred.

**Never confirm a private customer detail in public.** This is the
sharpest edge of this playbook, and it applies whether or not the
reviewer brought the detail up first. A reviewer can say whatever they
want about themselves; the business replying in a way that CONFIRMS they
were a patient, client, or customer of a sensitive kind of service — or
adds any specific about their case, their visit, their file, their
balance, their diagnosis, their matter — is a public disclosure the
business made, not the reviewer. Check `brain/compliance.md` before
finalizing any negative reply; lean on this hardest for a health,
medical, mental-health, legal, or financial business, where confirming so
much as "yes, you were a patient here" in public can be its own real
problem, independent of whatever the review itself said. When in doubt,
keep the reply generic enough that it would be true and safe under any
interpretation of what the reviewer described.

**Move it offline — to a REAL contact channel.** Give the reviewer one
clear next step: a real phone number or email address, taken from
`brain/business.md` or `setup.md`, never invented. "Please reach out to
us directly" with nothing real behind it is a hollow gesture; a real way
to reach a real person is what actually gives the reviewer, and the
reader, somewhere to go. Nothing real on file: use
`[PLACEHOLDER: a real way for a customer to reach the business directly]`
and say so, rather than writing "DM us" or "give us a call" with no
number attached.

**No compensation, ever, unless the owner explicitly said so.** The
default reply never offers a refund, a discount, a redo, or any other
compensation. If the owner said, for this batch or this specific review,
to offer something concrete: say exactly that and nothing more generous.
Otherwise, the offer is always the same — talk to us directly, not a deal
struck in public.

**Never claim the problem is already fixed.** "We've addressed this with
the team," "this has already been corrected," "this won't happen again" —
none of these go in a reply unless the owner told you, specifically, that
it is true. A promise made in public and later shown to be false is worse
than no promise at all.

**The shape, short:** acknowledge → (if there is one true, non-defensive
line worth adding, add it) → the real offline contact → a short, calm
sign-off. Three or four sentences is a normal ceiling. This is not the
place to tell the business's whole side of the story.

**A review that is really a support issue** (a refund, a billing
mistake, an order problem) still gets this playbook's short public reply
— the underlying problem is not solved here, in public, by this skill.
Flag it to the owner as support-side and move on.

**A review that looks fake, malicious, or from someone who was never
actually a customer** still does not get argued with in public. State
only what the owner has confirmed as fact, stay just as calm and short as
any other negative reply, and note in the item that this one might be
worth the owner reporting to Google directly — that reporting step is the
owner's to take, not something this skill does.

## Solicitation rules — Google's own policies, plainly

These are not house preferences. They are Google's stated policies for
businesses asking for reviews, and this skill follows them with no
exceptions, regardless of what an owner asks for in a given run.

**No review gating.** Filtering who gets asked to leave a public review
based on how happy they seem — for instance, a form that privately routes
an unhappy answer to an internal email and only sends a happy answer on
to the public review link — is against Google's policies. Every real
customer gets the same path to leaving a review, whatever they are likely
to say.

**No incentives, of any kind.** Offering a discount, a free item, an
entry into a drawing, or anything else in exchange for a review is
against Google's policies — this holds even when the incentive is
offered for "an honest review" regardless of rating, not only for a
positive one. A review with a reward attached to it is not treated as an
honest review by Google's own rules, whatever the intent behind offering
it was.

**Ask everyone, not just the customers you think will say something
nice.** This is the plain, positive version of "no gating" — the healthy
version of asking is a wide net, not a curated one. It is also just more
honest: a profile's reviews mean something to a future customer only if
they were not pre-selected.

**Why this matters beyond Google's rules.** Review gating and
incentivized reviews are also drawing real regulatory attention in a
growing number of places — this is not only a platform-terms risk.
Neither this skill, nor GrowOS generally, gives legal advice; if a
specific plan feels like it is close to a line, that is worth a real
conversation with someone qualified, not a guess made here.
