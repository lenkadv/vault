# Webinar structure — the canon, generalized

Mined from the owner's own real, shipped webinar work — a live 80-minute
event, built, rehearsed, and tested against simulated buyer personas before
air — not a generic webinar-marketing course template. Every proportion and
structural rule below survived multiple real revisions; where a rule changed
between drafts, the version below is the one that actually shipped.

**On illustrations in this file.** Where a worked example is useful, this
file builds a fresh, fictional one rather than reusing the mined material's
own — the source's real chapters, stories, and lines belong to the business
they were mined from. A handful of genuinely generic, already-scrubbed craft
lines (no product, no price, no person) are kept as short examples in
`references/cta-choreography.md` instead, each marked "Example" the same way
`vsl-write`'s structure reference marks its own.

## Quick reference: the shape at a glance

Baseline: an 80-minute show, excluding the untimed pre-show hold.

| # | Segment | Minutes (of 80) | Share | Macro-role |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| 1 | The Open (housekeeping opens it) | 9 | 11.5% | Hook, credibility, calibration |
| 2 | Teaching Chapter 1 | 18 | 22.5% | Largest chapter — the core value promise |
| 3 | Teaching Chapter 2 | 13 | 16% | Second promise |
| 4 | Teaching Chapter 3 | 6 | 7.5% | Third promise, deliberately short |
| 5 | Recap + Gift | 3 | 4% | Value close + free deliverable |
| 6 | The Pitch | 17 | 21% | The offer, fully structured |
| 7 | Q&A as close | 14 | 17.5% | Scripted objection handling, not improv |

**Teaching (Open + 3 chapters) ≈ 57.5% of runtime. The ask (Recap+Gift +
Pitch + Q&A) ≈ 42.5%.** The single sharpest fact in this shape: a
"free-value-first" webinar is not a short pitch bolted onto a long lesson.
The close — recap, pitch, and Q&A combined — holds at roughly two-fifths of
the whole show, every time this shape was revised. Treat "teaching ≈ 55-60% /
offer-and-close ≈ 40-45%" as the canonical split for any length.

**Chapter 1 gets the most real estate, always.** Generalized rule: whichever
promise is EASIEST to prove with a clean, fast, side-by-side before/after
goes first and earns roughly 40% of all teaching time — it is what carries
the audience's trust into every harder-to-prove promise that follows it.

## Scaling to a length target

Multiply the total minutes by each segment's exact fraction of 80 below
(not the rounded percentages above, to avoid compounding rounding error),
then round each result to a whole or half minute and true up the total
against the two largest, most flexible blocks — Chapter 1 and the Pitch —
rather than shaving a little off every segment.

| Segment | Fraction of total |
|---|---|
| The Open | 9/80 |
| Chapter 1 | 18/80 |
| Chapter 2 | 13/80 |
| Chapter 3 | 6/80 |
| Recap + Gift | 3/80 |
| The Pitch | 17/80 |
| Q&A close | 14/80 |

**Worked example at 45 minutes:**

| Segment | Minutes |
|---|---:|
| The Open | 5 |
| Chapter 1 | 10 |
| Chapter 2 | 7 |
| Chapter 3 | 3 |
| Recap + Gift | 2 |
| The Pitch | 10 |
| Q&A close | 8 |
| **Total** | **45** |

**A floor exists.** Below roughly 30-40 minutes total, Chapter 3 compresses
to 2-3 minutes — enough to gesture at a belief shift, not enough to actually
land one. When a length target lands there, consider merging Chapter 2 and
Chapter 3's promises into a single chapter (a 2-chapter shape) rather than
forcing three chapters into a space where the third one can't do its job.
This is this skill's own craft judgment, not a mined rule — say so plainly
when the call gets made, and let the owner weigh in if the merge changes
which promise gets cut.

**Stretching longer (a 90-minute or half-day masterclass) grows teaching, not
the close.** Extra time belongs to the teaching chapters — a deeper demo, a
second worked example, more room in the "how" progression. Do not let the
close shrink below its ~40-45% floor even at a longer length: the rehearsal
evidence below (Section 5) shows the close is exactly where under-rehearsal
and under-budgeting happen first, regardless of total length.

## 1. The generative method: belief chains

Before a single slide or line of script gets written, every teaching chapter
gets built as a **belief chain** — four short, explicit statements:

1. **They currently believe:** the audience's limiting belief, in one plain
   sentence — the thing they think is just "how it is."
2. **Break it with:** the specific sequence of teaching, proof, and analogy
   that dismantles it. Never a bare assertion — always a demonstration.
3. **New belief:** the one-sentence replacement belief the audience should
   leave holding.
4. **Self-challenge verdict:** yes, no, or conditional — does this chapter
   actually land the new belief, or does it just describe it? A conditional
   verdict names exactly what it depends on (a demo landing cleanly, a proof
   point holding up), so it can be checked again once that piece is real.

This is the single most reusable device in the whole method. It forces
naming the audience's actual objection before writing a word of teaching, and
it builds in its own quality gate — "did this land, yes or no" — instead of
assuming good copy automatically lands. Fill in all four parts for every
promise/chapter BEFORE drafting a single slide or script line.

**A verdict of "no" or "conditional" is a finding, not a formality.** In the
mined material, one chapter's own verdict came back conditional on a later
chart landing cleanly — and that flag turned out to mark a real structural
risk, not a false alarm. Treat a non-"yes" verdict the same way here: either
fix the chapter's proof or demo plan now, or carry it forward explicitly as a
known risk for the owner's own rehearsal to test. Never quietly round it up
to "yes" just to keep moving.

**Illustration (fictional and generic — built fresh for this file, not
mined):**

> A chapter teaching "you can batch a week of content in one sitting."
> - **They currently believe:** good content has to be made fresh, in the
>   moment, or it feels fake.
> - **Break it with:** a side-by-side of a same-day post and a
>   batched-a-week-earlier post, shown unlabeled, asking the room to guess
>   which one was batched.
> - **New belief:** freshness lives in the writing, not the clock — batching
>   changes nothing the audience can actually detect.
> - **Self-challenge verdict:** conditional — depends on the side-by-side
>   genuinely being indistinguishable. If the batched post reads noticeably
>   flatter, the chapter's whole claim collapses. Test this before air.

## 2. The ordered segments

### Pre-show / hold (untimed, before the advertised start)

A holding screen, the presenter greeting arrivals by name, zero content
pressure. *Must accomplish:* absorb latecomers without punishing people who
showed up on time; establish warmth before any teaching begins.

### Housekeeping (opens the Open segment, ~1 slide, no separate time budget)

Logistics only: expected length, replay availability if one exists, a
one-line tease that a free deliverable exists near the end (no description,
no timing yet), and an explicit chat-response protocol — something like "I'll
check chat at set moments, so I'm not ignoring you between beats." This line
matters most for a solo presenter with no one else watching chat live: say it
plainly rather than pretending to real-time attentiveness the format can't
actually deliver.

### The Open (~10-15% of runtime)

In order:

1. **Title/promise, stated once, slowly** — like it's meant, not rushed past.
2. **The payoff proof** — a concrete, finished result, shown or described
   BEFORE any teaching begins, proving the end-state is real. Open with
   evidence, not promise language: show the destination before teaching the
   route. This beat exists because a rehearsal pass is what actually catches
   whether an opening lands as evidence or falls flat (see Section 5,
   below) — not something to guess right on the first draft.
3. **A gift tease** — one sentence, seconds long: a free deliverable exists
   later. No description, no price, no urgency. Just a reason to stay.
4. **Presenter credibility, kept short** (30-75 seconds) — one or two
   concrete facts, not a résumé, reframed as "the only credential that
   matters for the next hour," tied to a mission, not a list of
   accomplishments.
5. **An honest expectation-setter** — a direct statement that the method is
   real work, not a push-button fantasy, paired immediately with the payoff
   ("one person can now do what used to take a team"). Sets a hype ceiling in
   the same breath as the ambition claim.
6. **Audience self-calibration** — a numbered poll, kept to 3 options (a 4th,
   most-advanced-sounding option mostly served ego and added decision
   fatigue in testing). Every bucket gets told, explicitly, it is a valid,
   useful place to be.
7. **Myth-busting / the two failure modes** — name two opposite ways the
   audience gets this wrong, confessed as mistakes the presenter made too.
8. **The one shared success test** — a single, stated-once evaluation rule
   the ENTIRE show gets judged against (does it grow revenue, cut cost, or
   give time back — if not, it's a toy). Every later value-checkpoint calls
   back to this exact test instead of re-justifying value from scratch. This
   is the single most load-bearing sentence in the whole show.
9. **A credible proof-of-concept** — one or two real, dated artifacts with
   real, even modest, numbers, banked before teaching starts, to earn trust
   for the bigger claims still to come.

*Must accomplish:* hook with evidence, earn the right to teach by being
honest about difficulty, calibrate to a mixed-awareness room without
insulting either end, and install the one metric everything else gets
measured against.

### The chapter template (all three teaching chapters share this shape)

Each promise becomes an imperative-titled chapter — a command the audience
can act on, never a "secret." Every chapter follows the same seven-step
internal shape:

1. **Chapter divider** — the promise stated as an instruction, visually
   distinct (dark/high-contrast) from the surrounding content, marking a
   gear change.
2. **Name the current failure honestly** — restate the chapter's belief-chain
   "they currently believe" step on-slide, often with the presenter
   confessing they did the same thing.
3. **The single reframe** — one repeatable, almost sloganizable sentence that
   is the chapter's entire payload. Everything else in the chapter serves
   this one line.
4. **A structured "how," cheapest-first** — a numbered progression (2-4
   steps) from the easiest zero-cost version of the technique to its most
   powerful form. Every level must be doable with tools the audience already
   has — no purchase required yet.
5. **A free, copy-paste artifact** — a literal script, prompt, or checklist
   the audience can use during the session, given with zero gate, styled so
   it visually reads as "this is FOR YOU." Budget a few real seconds of dead
   air here so people can actually capture it.
6. **A proof run** — a real, dated before/after or side-by-side, never a bare
   claim. When the proof needs more than one image to read clearly, split it
   across several consecutive slides rather than cramming it onto one —
   readability at stream/small-screen resolution is the reason.
7. **A closing "money echo"** — a short card translating the chapter's
   teaching back into the ONE shared success test from the Open, paired with
   a chat beat roughly half the time. This is the chapter's exit ramp and the
   connective tissue to the next chapter.

**Per-chapter differences (proportions and role only — the actual belief each
chapter breaks is this business's own, drawn from its methodology and its
audience's real objections, never copied from another business's chapters):**

- **Chapter 1 — largest (~40% of all teaching time).** Gets the most real
  estate because it is the easiest promise to prove with a clean, fast
  side-by-side — the single most persuasive proof format there is.
- **Chapter 2 — second.** Where the mechanism connects to real tooling or
  process, usually with an explicit human-approval checkpoint (nothing ships
  without a yes/no from a person). Its belief-chain "break it with" step
  should concede the skeptic's best rebuttal in the presenter's own words
  before anyone else can raise it — see `cta-choreography.md`'s "objection
  pre-handles."
- **Chapter 3 — shortest, deliberately.** Carries a SMALL, personal version
  of the show's urgency argument; the LARGER, market-level version of the
  same argument relocates to open the Pitch (see below) — which is exactly
  why Chapter 3 can stay short without feeling thin.

### Recap + Gift (~4% of runtime)

1. A rapid, high-energy recap — "everything in sixty seconds," confident and
   warm, landing like a coach who already knows the room learned something,
   not a hype-man beat.
2. **The free deliverable, revealed.** Strongest version: the gift is itself
   made by the same method just taught — a self-demonstrating proof that the
   method works.
3. **The permission bridge** — an explicit, genuine "you have what you came
   for, you're free to go" statement, paired with a low-pressure invitation
   for the curious to stay a few more minutes. A trust move, deliberately
   positioned against the harder-pressure alternatives named in
   `cta-choreography.md`.

*Must accomplish:* deliver the free-value promise completely and visibly, and
hand the audience a genuine, non-manipulative choice point before the pitch
begins.

### The Pitch (~21% of runtime — a fully structured arc, not an afterthought)

The internal order, reveal through the CTA slide, is craft this skill applies
while scripting; the complete sequence lives in
`references/cta-choreography.md` — read it before drafting this segment.

### Q&A as close (~17.5% of runtime — scripted, not improvised)

Every question gets treated as a restated objection and answered
agree-amplify-reframe. A pre-written seed bank of the most common objections
fires if chat goes quiet, so the segment is never dead air and never purely
reactive. At least one answer uses the asker's name. It closes with a
distinct, low-pressure "two paths, both fine" statement — worded differently
from the fork that closed the pitch slide, so the ending doesn't feel
repeated — then a held silent end card.

**No live chat to read from (a pre-recorded or evergreen replay)?** The seed
bank stops being a fallback and becomes the whole segment: frame it as "the
questions people always ask" rather than "what's coming in from chat," and
script it with the same agree-amplify-reframe shape and the same closing
fork.

## 3. Engagement beats

Chat/interaction beats are deliberately sparse and clustered at SEGMENT
BOUNDARIES — end of the Open, end of each teaching chapter, at the CTA —
rather than scattered continuously. State the cadence to the audience up
front at Housekeeping (see above); it is an explicit accommodation for a
single presenter with no one else watching chat live.

The settled beat list:

1. **Early warm-up consent check** (early in the Open) — a single-word "type
   X if that sounds fair" beat, right after the format gets explained. A
   low-stakes first rep at participation and a soft consent check on the
   framing.
2. **Self-diagnostic poll** (Open/Teaching boundary) — 3 options, not 4.
   Calibrates delivery live and makes every awareness segment in the room
   feel named. Seed a callback line here for later use.
3. **An open-ended "name your biggest [pain point]" question** (early, or at
   Chapter 1's close) — read answers aloud BY NAME and plan, from the moment
   the question is written, to call them back at least twice more later
   (once mid-teaching, once in the bonus/pitch section). Collecting a piece
   of the audience's own material and reusing it 2-3 times later is one of
   the single most copy-paste-able devices here — plan the callback chain
   when writing the question; don't wait to discover one later.
4. **Comprehension check** ("type YES if you see why...") at the close of
   Chapter 1, paired with its money-echo card — both engagement and a tacit
   buy-in checkpoint before advancing.
5. **A forward-looking question** at the close of Chapter 2, designed to
   plant material for a specific later bonus/offer beat.
6. **Zero-friction "drop an emoji" beat** exactly at the CTA moment — the
   lowest possible commitment gesture, timed to the instant of decision.

**Engagement is a property, not its own template.** Any slide, whatever its
visual pattern, can carry an interaction beat (see
`references/slides-outline-format.md`'s `chat-beat` pattern versus its
optional `CHAT BEAT:` field) — a chat prompt is something layered onto a
slide, not a slide type by itself.

**Live demonstrations** (up to two, each capped 30-60 seconds): one inside
Chapter 1, optionally one inside Chapter 2. Hard rule: the static slide is
ALWAYS also the fallback. If a live demo misbehaves or time runs short,
narrate over the same static slide with no visible seam. Never make the
show's success depend on a live demo working — design the "just narrate it"
fallback into the same slide from the start.

## 4. The kill list

Check every line of every draft against this before it ships:

- **No fake urgency.** No manufactured countdown, no "spots filling," no
  invented deadline. A real deadline, once it exists, is spoken exactly
  once, in the presenter's own voice, and never printed on more than one
  surface (see `cta-choreography.md`, "Honest urgency").
- **No printed countdown timer inside the offer/pitch section.** (A pre-show
  hold screen counting down to the actual start time is a different,
  harmless thing — that one is fine.)
- **No repeated calls to buy scattered through the teaching.** There is
  exactly one full pitch arc, near the end. Earlier mentions are a single
  seed, never a sales beat.
- **No dollar-total stacking.** The full offer recap is read once, top to
  bottom, without adding up an inflated "total value" figure.
- **No hype-adjective inflation.** Words like "revolutionary,"
  "game-changing," "insane," "once-in-a-lifetime," or "guaranteed to change
  your life" don't survive a pass — say what's true, plainly, and let the
  proof carry the weight. Keep a written banned-word list of this specific
  business's own worst habits (a maintained list catches recurring tells a
  one-time read misses) and check every draft against it, every revision.
- **No internal production label left visible on a slide** (a pattern name,
  a beat number, an internal note) — a slide is a clean thought, not a
  dashboard.
- **No invented bonus.** A bonus stated in the stack is real and actually
  ships. Reconcile the bonus list against the literal current offer every
  single pass — a stack that quietly drifts from what's actually being sold
  is a common, easy-to-miss failure.
- **No stronger guarantee than what's actually true**, and nothing implied
  beyond what `brain/compliance.md` clears.

On-slide reading-grade discipline and the hard split between what's shown and
what's said live in `references/slides-outline-format.md` — read it before
drafting the slides outline.

## 5. Rehearsal as diagnostic

A script is not finished when it's written; it is finished when it survives
being said out loud, once, start to finish, against the clock. These are
generalized findings from a full rehearsal pass, tested against several
simulated buyer personas before the settled shape locked. Hand these to the
owner as what to listen for in their own rehearsal — this skill drafts the
words; only the owner can rehearse the delivery.

1. **Time-box the best story.** An unbroken personal story running past
   about 3-4 minutes is where attention measurably drops first, in every
   tested persona. Cap any single anecdote, and cut it in rehearsal the
   moment it runs long — don't plan to "tighten it in post."
2. **Don't bury the best proof as a throwaway aside.** The single most
   surprising, most persuasive proof point in a script is wasted if it's
   delivered as a quick joke. Identify the ONE most surprising, provable
   claim and open with it, deliberately and slowly.
3. **One new concept per beat, cleanly defined.** Introducing several new
   terms back-to-back creates a silent floor for the least technical person
   in the room — watch for a presenter re-explaining the same idea several
   times in one take; that is the tell. Rehearse a definition as its own
   timed beat, not a fast aside.
4. **The close is where rehearsal discipline collapses first.** Price,
   guarantee, objection-handling, and the CTA combined often get only
   seconds of real airtime in a first rehearsal, while buildup and
   testimonials eat the clock. Protect a real time budget for the close and
   rehearse it out loud on purpose — the teaching always feels safer to talk
   about than the ask.
5. **"I don't know what to say here" is a scripting bug, not a delivery
   problem.** Any beat where the presenter goes blank on a rehearsal take
   needs a fully written line before the next pass — never leave a
   monetization or transition beat to improvisation.
6. **Capture the presenter's real-time self-commentary, not just the
   content.** A presenter narrating live edits to themselves mid-rehearsal
   ("energy's low right now," "I'm not sure what point I'm making") is a
   higher-signal edit cue than a cold read of the transcript. Listen to the
   whole rehearsal, not just what was supposed to be said.
7. **Test against more than one persona; expect disagreement.** Different
   buyer types will diverge sharply on where a show works and where it
   breaks. Don't optimize a single pass for one idealized viewer — a "smooth
   it all out" edit misses type-specific failures.
8. **A missing transition creates a visible seam.** A presenter audibly
   groping for how to move to the next idea is a scripting gap, not a
   delivery slip. Script explicit bridge lines between every major beat.
9. **Compress scene-setting, protect the payoff.** An extended honesty aside
   or a tangential story that runs long in isolation usually folds into one
   sentence riding inside a neighboring beat just fine — it doesn't need its
   own slide.
10. **Design the fallback into the demo slide itself** (see Section 3,
    above) — never make a beat's success depend on a live demo working.
11. **An unpaid callback is a defect.** Any open loop — "I'll tell you why
    later," "remember this" — that never actually pays off is a scripting
    bug to fix before air, not a stylistic slip. Track every open loop
    created and confirm it closes (see `cta-choreography.md`, "The
    open-loop device").
12. **Silence is a rehearsed skill, not an absence of content.** Specify an
    exact pause length at the highest-stakes moments (after the price
    reveal, after the closing gut-check question) — "pause meaningfully" is
    not specific enough to actually rehearse or hand to someone else.
13. **Re-verify the offer against its actual, current, live state — every
    pass.** A rehearsal pass is also a fact-check pass: reconcile every claim
    about the offer against what's really being sold right now, not an
    earlier draft of it.

**Further craft, drawn from how this shape evolved across real revisions:**
once a beat repeats 2-3 times, name it and treat it as a fixed component
rather than a bespoke decision each time; introduce one shared success metric
early and point back at it instead of re-arguing value from scratch; save the
strongest visual register (dark/high-contrast) for gear-changes and proof,
not a constant tone held throughout; prefer captured, dated proof over a live
demo wherever liveness itself is not the point; default to fewer
self-identification buckets in a poll, not more; consolidate urgency to one
spoken instance and audit for it creeping back onto a second slide; pair
every paid technique with a free, zero-setup version the audience can run
today; audit for the same story or metaphor being paid off twice under two
different framings and cut one; and promote an objection from reactive Q&A
into a scripted slide once real evidence (not a guess) shows it is common,
not rare.

## What generalizes, and what must never leave this file

**Carries into every business's webinar:** the seven-segment shape and its
proportions; belief chains before scripting; the chapter template's seven
internal steps; Chapter 1 as the largest, easiest-to-prove-first chapter;
Chapter 3's urgency split (small/personal here, large/market at the pitch
open); the engagement-beat placement rule and beat list; the kill list; the
rehearsal lessons.

**Never carries over:** any product name, price, bonus name, personal story,
named customer, specific performance number, presenter bio fact, specific
vendor or tool choice, exact date or deadline, or literal banned-word list,
brand color, or typeface from the material this canon was mined from. This
business's webinar uses this business's own real material — from
`brain/business.md`, `brain/audience.md`, `brain/proof/`, `brain/stories/`,
and `brain/methodology.md` — never a detail borrowed from an illustration in
this file.
