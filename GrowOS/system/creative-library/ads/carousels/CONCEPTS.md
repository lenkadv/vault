# Carousel concepts

A carousel concept in this library is one idea told across four or five square images that visibly
continue into each other: the same scene, the same subject, the same set of objects, moving through
a small story or transformation. Each concept commits fully to one visual world, either photoreal
(it could pass as a real photograph) or proudly artificial (obviously a crafted illustration or
render), never something in between. Words on screen are kept to almost nothing, because the story
is meant to be read by the eye in a couple of seconds while someone scrolls, not stopped and read
like a paragraph. No logo, URL, or CTA button ever appears inside any slide, the ad unit around the
images carries that. Because the story only works in a fixed sequence, these get uploaded to Meta
with the card order locked and never left to Meta's automatic reordering.

Each concept below includes its visual conceit, a beat-by-beat plan you can hand to an AI image
model yourself, and a judgment call on what kind of offer it fits. Pick the mechanic that matches
the point you're trying to make, not just the prettiest option.

## 1. One Desk, One Day

**The visual conceit:** One continuous panoramic photograph of a single desk, shot straight on, that
gets cropped into slides reading left to right like a comic strip. The far left is 8am chaos, the
far right is 5pm calm, and the light itself warms up gradually across the frame. It works as a
scroll-stopper because it reads as a single real photograph of a messy desk slowly getting its act
together, not four separate images stitched together, so the eye follows it the way it would follow
a time-lapse. Pole: photoreal. Connecting device: one unbroken desk and light gradient running under
everything, plus a small rubber duck that quietly migrates closer to the keyboard in each crop and a
desk clock that ticks forward from morning to evening.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. Far left of the desk, 8am, cool blue-grey light: a cluttered monitor, a scatter of sticky notes,
   several half-empty coffee mugs, tangled cables, loose papers, a tipped pen jar, a small clock
   reading early morning. The duck sits furthest from the keyboard here.
2. A little further right, still morning but calming: one mug fewer, cables starting to coil, papers
   thinning out, the light beginning to warm. The duck has moved a step closer to the keyboard.
3. Further right again, afternoon: the desk is noticeably tidier, warmer light, fewer objects
   cluttering the surface. The duck is closer still.
4. Far right, 5pm, golden light: one clean mug, a small plant, a single tidy folder on the screen, a
   clock reading early evening, the duck now resting right beside the keyboard. A short line near the
   top of this slide notes that it's one desk running one system.

**What offer types it suits:** Anything whose core promise is turning a chaotic workday into a calm
one, especially a service that quietly does the tidying in the background. It fits offers built
around daily or ongoing operational relief rather than a single deliverable. For Summit Books & Tax,
this is the natural shape for "receipts and spreadsheets pile up in the morning, books are
reconciled and calm by the time you close the laptop." It's less suited to a one-off creative
product, since the payoff here is about a recurring state changing, not a single finished thing.

## 2. One Lit Window

**The visual conceit:** A slow pull-back, one camera move stretched across four slides: it starts as
an extreme macro shot on a glowing screen and ends as a wide shot of a whole street at dusk, with
that one office window still lit as a tiny warm dot. It works because it reframes "the work is done"
as a small, human, almost cinematic moment rather than a checklist getting ticked off, and the
zoom-out itself is satisfying to watch unfold slide by slide. Pole: photoreal. Connecting device: the
exact same warm glow and dusk color palette persists through every zoom level, so the eye never
loses the thread even as the frame gets wider and wider.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. Extreme macro on a monitor screen at the end of the day, glowing warm against a dim room: the
   screen shows a single clean word confirming the day's work got its final yes.
2. Pull back one step to the whole desk beside a window at dusk: same warm glow on the screen, but
   the chair is now empty and pushed back, because whoever was sitting there already left for the
   evening.
3. Pull back again to outside the house at dusk: one upstairs window glows warm, the same office from
   before, while a different downstairs window shows soft family silhouettes moving around a kitchen.
4. Pull back to a wide street view at blue hour: the house is now small among trees and neighbors,
   that one office window just a tiny warm dot. A short line near the bottom notes that the work
   shipped and the person is already at dinner.

**What offer types it suits:** Any offer whose real promise is time and presence back, not just
output. It's built for solo owners who are the entire marketing department, where the win isn't "one
more newsletter went out" but "I got my evening back." The Steady Coach is the clean fit here: a
single-person business where the whole pitch is reclaiming the hours that used to go to writing
posts and emails after the paying work is done.

## 3. The Vending Machine

**The visual conceit:** A cute, toy-like vending machine stocked with shrink-wrapped packets, each
labeled for a different marketing deliverable. It works because a vending machine is an instantly
readable metaphor for "put in a small effort, get a bundle of finished things out," and the classic
"stuck item" gag gives it a beat of tension and relief that a straight product shot never would.
Pole: proudly artificial, a clean toy-like 3D render, never trying to pass as a photo. Connecting
device: the same machine, same coil rows, same packet labels across every slide, tracked through
coin insertion, drop, stuck-item, jackpot, and overflow.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. The machine stands front and center, its rows stocked with neatly wrapped packets labeled for
   different deliverable types (a newsletter, an ad round, launch emails, a carousel, proof). A coin
   representing one afternoon's effort is being inserted.
2. The first packet drops into the tray with a satisfying thud, its coil now empty, the rest still
   waiting in their rows.
3. Comedic hitch: a second packet hangs stuck at the edge of its coil, the familiar "oh no" vending
   machine moment, while the first packet already sits collected in the tray below.
4. A gentle nudge on the machine frees the stuck packet, and two more tumble down at once in a happy
   cascade, more landing than was expected.
5. The tray now overflows with the full pile of packets, being scooped up joyfully. A small display on
   the machine notes no repeat coins are needed, closing on the idea that this was a single purchase,
   not a subscription.

**What offer types it suits:** Bundled, productized offers where a customer pays once and gets many
distinct pieces of output, especially when the "more than you paid for" feeling is part of the pitch.
It fits a done-for-you offer with several separate deliverables inside it rather than a single
ongoing service. Willow & Wren fits this shape well for a seasonal content bundle: one purchase,
several finished pieces of marketing coming out the other side.

## 4. The Domino Chain

**The visual conceit:** One continuous panoramic photograph of a row of dominoes on a desk, split
into slides so the fall reads left to right like a story. It opens on a single fingertip tipping the
first tile and ends with the run splitting into three branches that land in a final formation. It
works because a domino chain is the most intuitive visual shorthand there is for "one small setup
action causes a chain reaction," and watching a single fingertip cause that much downstream motion is
satisfying on its own. Pole: photoreal. Connecting device: one unbroken domino run across a single
desk, with individual tiles engraved with single words that mark the story's progress.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. Far left, a fingertip gently tips the very first domino, engraved with a small note about one
   setup session. The tile is just beginning to lean.
2. The fall continues, weaving between ordinary desk objects like a keyboard and a coffee mug. A few
   tiles along the way carry single words marking what got set up.
3. The single run splits into three parallel branches fanning outward, each branch tagged for a
   different channel: email, social, ads.
4. Far right, the three branches arrive together and the final tiles land upright, spelling out a
   short phrase about marketing running on repeat. A short line above the desk notes that this only
   had to be set up once.

**What offer types it suits:** Automation and "set it up once, it keeps running" offers, where the
value is one initial setup action multiplying into several ongoing channels without repeat manual
work. It's a poor fit for anything that needs regular hands-on input, since the whole metaphor is
about not touching it again. Summit Books & Tax suits this well for an onboarding-once, runs-forever
bookkeeping system, where a single setup call cascades into weekly reports across several areas of
the business without further intervention.

## 5. The Booster Pack

**The visual conceit:** A sealed foil trading-card pack, torn open in glossy hero-lit product
photography, revealing individually named cards with rarity tiers, ending on a collector's binder
page filling up with them. It works because it reframes a long list of features as something people
already find inherently exciting to open and collect, turning "here are fifty things this includes"
into an unboxing moment instead of a spec sheet. Pole: proudly artificial, an obviously crafted
premium studio render, not a photo. Connecting device: the same foil pack and desk mat across every
slide, tracked through tearing open, pulling the first card, fanning out more cards, and finally
filling a binder page.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. A sealed foil pack lies on a premium desk mat, labeled for a first series of marketing skills,
   glossy studio lighting and a prismatic sheen across the foil.
2. The pack is being torn open, foil peeling back mid-tear, one card just beginning to slide free.
3. A single glossy card is pulled out and held up, named for one specific skill, with a rarity tag and
   a short flavor line describing what it does.
4. Three more cards are fanned out, each named for a different skill with its own rarity tier, holo
   shimmer across all of them.
5. A collector's binder page is filling up with these cards, most slots already filled, one more being
   slotted in by hand. A short line notes the full set fits in one box, no further packs needed.

**What offer types it suits:** Offers with a large number of distinct modules, templates, or
capabilities, where the challenge is making abundance feel exciting rather than overwhelming. It
suits a software or template library offer better than a single-deliverable service. The Steady Coach
fits this shape for a course or template library with many individual lessons or assets, where
"there are fifty of these and they're all included" needs to feel like a collection worth having, not
a wall of text.

## 6. The Marketing Reading

**The visual conceit:** An ornate tarot reading, three cards flipped face up one at a time over
candlelight, each revealing a named pattern in how the business's marketing is currently stuck before
resolving into the upright, hopeful card. It works because a reading format gives permission to name
an uncomfortable truth (procrastination, isolation, burnout) in a way that feels insightful and a
little fun rather than accusatory, which is a much softer way to surface a real problem than saying it
outright. Pole: proudly artificial, an original illustrated deck in a classic tarot style, never
photographic. Connecting device: the same velvet cloth, candle, and gold-foil deck art across every
slide, with the face-up card advancing one position to the right each time.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. Three cards lie face down in a neat row on dark velvet, candlelight glowing beside them, a title
   above framing this as a reading of the business's marketing.
2. The left card flips face up and reversed: a weary figure trudges uphill under an overloaded yoke of
   tasks, named for being the single bottleneck everything routes through.
3. The middle card flips face up: a founder stands alone on a peak holding a lantern crammed full of
   ideas that never made it out into the world, named for working in total isolation.
4. The right card flips face up and upright: a calm figure approves a page while a small machine ships
   finished work onto a growing stack. A short fortune line beneath contrasts the upright reading
   against the reversed one, approving and letting it ship versus doing everything alone forever.

**What offer types it suits:** Diagnostic or assessment-led offers, where the first step is naming
what's actually broken before proposing a fix, such as a free audit or a discovery call as the entry
point. It works less well for an offer that's purely execution with no diagnosis step. Summit Books &
Tax could open a campaign with this to name the actual reason a client's books are always three
weeks behind, before the offer itself resolves it.

## 7. Paper Airplanes

**The visual conceit:** A desk buried in half-written, crossed-out drafts that magically fold
themselves into paper airplanes, fly in formation across the room, and land stacked neatly in a tray.
It works because the transformation from "pile of abandoned drafts" to "finished and filed" is shown
as something that happens on its own, with no visible effort, which lands the point about
unfinished ideas getting completed without anyone having to sit and do it. Pole: photoreal room with
one obviously magical element, the self-folding paper, so the setting is believable but the action
is knowingly impossible. Connecting device: the exact same golden-hour desk, lamp, and window across
every slide, with the drafts tracked from chaos through self-folding, flight, and landing.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. A desk is buried under a mountain of half-written drafts: scribbled pages, crossed-out lines,
   crumpled balls, spilling onto the floor, all in warm golden-hour light.
2. The drafts begin folding themselves into paper airplanes mid-air, creases forming with no hands
   involved, one page frozen halfway between a scribbled draft and a finished plane.
3. A small formation of finished paper airplanes banks gracefully across the room in the golden light,
   one already nosing toward the edge of the frame.
4. The airplanes land and nest neatly into a tray labeled for review, the desk now clear and calm. A
   short line near the bottom notes that half-finished ideas are now finished and filed.

**What offer types it suits:** Offers centered on turning raw, unfinished material into something
polished and ready to publish, especially writing or content-heavy offers where the customer's own
input is messy notes rather than a clean brief. The Steady Coach fits well here: half-formed lesson
ideas and voice notes turning into finished, ready-to-send content without the coach having to sit
down and write it themselves.

## 8. The Claw Machine

**The visual conceit:** A classic arcade claw machine, except the claw grips every prize firmly and
never drops it, closing on someone hugging a full armful of wins. It works by directly calling out
the shared experience of the rigged arcade claw that always slips at the last second, then subverting
it, so the relief of "it actually works" lands harder because everyone already expects the opposite.
Pole: proudly artificial, a glossy toy-like 3D render, not a photo. Connecting device: the same
glowing cabinet and candy-colored plush orbs, each labeled for a skill, tracked through the grip, the
delivery, and a second grab happening immediately after.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. A claw machine cabinet is packed with plush toy orbs, several near the front tagged with skill
   names, a chrome claw poised above and a hand resting on the joystick.
2. The claw descends and grips one orb firmly, no slipping, just beginning to lift it while the other
   labeled orbs stay nestled below.
3. That won orb now sits delivered in the prize chute, while inside the cabinet the claw has already
   gripped a second orb, an eager immediate second win.
4. Someone hugs a full armful of won orbs at a cozy desk. A short line contrasts this with the usual
   arcade experience and notes it was a single purchase.

**What offer types it suits:** Reliability-positioned offers, especially ones competing against a
category with a bad reputation for inconsistency, like agencies or freelancers who sometimes deliver
and sometimes don't. It's a strong fit wherever the sales conversation includes "will this actually
work every time." Willow & Wren fits this when the alternative being compared against is a string of
past freelancers or agencies that delivered unevenly.

## 9. One Move, Every Board

**The visual conceit:** A single calm person walking down a row of chessboards in a sunlit hall, one
board per channel, playing a decisive move at each while every opponent's chair sits empty. It works
because chess signals competence and control without needing to explain anything, and the empty
chairs quietly make the point that no one else needs to be in the room for this to run. Pole:
photoreal, shot like elegant editorial photography. Connecting device: the exact same sunlit hall,
long table, and calm player across every slide, tracked as he moves from board to board and the
finished boards pile up behind him.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. A long table holds a row of chessboards, each with a small placard naming a channel (email,
   content, ads visible), every opponent's chair empty on the far side. One calm person stands at the
   first board, mid-move.
2. A macro close-up on that first board: its pieces are quietly themed to the channel, the opponent's
   chair empty and blurred behind.
3. The same person has walked further down the table to a later board. The boards behind him are now
   finished in clean, resolved positions, with no one standing at them.
4. A wide shot from behind shows him walking away, having finished the whole row. Every board is
   resolved, every opponent chair still empty. A short line notes that one move was all it took to say
   yes.

**What offer types it suits:** Offers for a solo owner or a very small team who needs to run several
channels at once without hiring anyone. It fits offers pitched on replacing a whole team's worth of
channel-by-channel work with one person making the calls. The Steady Coach is the clean example: one
person approving decisions across email, content, and ads without needing a team seated at any of
those boards.

## 10. The Pit Stop

**The visual conceit:** A desk fused with a Formula 1 chassis screaming into a pit box, serviced by a
crew in seconds while the driver never stops working, then launches back out. It works because a pit
stop is a universally understood symbol of speed and precision under time pressure, and having the
driver keep typing through the whole service makes the point that the person never actually has to
stop what they're doing. Pole: proudly artificial, a glossy, dynamic 3D render, not a photo.
Connecting device: the same desk-car and the same calm character across every slide, tracked from
arrival through the service itself to the sign flipping and the car launching back out.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. A desk fused with an F1 car screams into the pit box with motion-blur streaks. The driver, helmet
   visor up, keeps typing on a laptop bolted to the desk, completely unbothered.
2. The car is stopped mid-service. A pit crew swarms it: topping off a coffee mug, swapping a tire
   labeled for a weekly deliverable, bolting on a wing labeled for this week's output.
3. A trackside sign flips from hold to ship, a pit board displays a fast service time, and the crew
   steps back as the driver sits ready.
4. The car accelerates away down the empty pit lane, papers settling in its wake. A counter in the
   scene tracks an ongoing week number. A short line notes the week got serviced while the driver never
   left the seat.

**What offer types it suits:** Recurring, cadence-driven service offers where speed and a repeatable
weekly rhythm are the pitch, more than a one-off project. It fits ongoing management or maintenance
work better than a single deliverable. Summit Books & Tax suits this shape for a weekly bookkeeping
cadence: the books get serviced fast, on a set rhythm, without the owner ever stepping away from
their own work to make it happen.

## 11. The Never-Ending Scarves

**The visual conceit:** A stage magician pulls an impossibly long, knotted chain of banners out of
one small folder, each banner labeled for a different deliverable, while a skeptic in the front row
slowly goes from arms-crossed to leaning in and won over. It works because it stages the exact
objection a buyer is thinking, "that's too much output from one small thing," and answers it inside
the story itself by converting a visible skeptic on camera rather than just claiming it in copy.
Pole: proudly artificial, a warm painterly illustrated stage scene, never photographic. Connecting
device: the same knotted banner chain running across every slide's border, plus the same magician,
folder, and skeptic tracked from the first pull through to the skeptic being won over.

**Beat-by-beat card plan:**
1. On a warm stage, a plain folder sits on a stool. A stylized magician pulls a knotted chain of
   banners up out of it, the first one named for a single deliverable type. A skeptic sits arms
   crossed in the front row, unimpressed.
2. The chain keeps coming, three more banners visible in sequence, each named for a different
   deliverable, the magician delighted at how much keeps emerging.
3. Focus shifts to the skeptic, now leaning forward, arms uncrossed, holding part of the chain and
   reading it closely, visibly won over.
4. The house lights come up. The chain now fills the aisle, and the folder on the stool is still
   visibly half full of more banners waiting. The skeptic is smiling. A short line closes on the idea
   that there's no trick and no extra hands involved, just a system, and that it was a single
   purchase.

**What offer types it suits:** Productized, done-for-you offers where the core claim is a large
volume of output from one small input, and where overcoming "that sounds too good to be true" is
part of the actual sales conversation. Willow & Wren fits well here for a case where a handful of
product photos turn into a full spread of marketing material, and the skeptic beat does the work of
addressing the doubt before the buyer has to voice it themselves.

## How to produce these

Each slide gets generated with an AI image model that supports reference-image continuity: generate
slide one, then feed that finished image back in as a reference when generating slide two so the
world, subject, and lighting stay consistent, and repeat that same feed-forward pattern through the
last slide in the sequence. Your AI image model of choice works for this (the kie.ai nano-banana image
model is one option that handles this kind of reference-image continuity well). Generate every slide
at a square 1:1 aspect ratio, then upload the finished set to Meta as a carousel creative in the exact
order it was planned. Never let Meta auto-order the cards, the whole point of the concept is a story
that only reads correctly in sequence.
