# Format library

Four formats ship at v1: checklist, guide, template/swipe pack, and
mini-course outline. That is a deliberate cut, not an oversight - 0.1 also
had a quiz, a case study, a calculator, and video training, and none of
those made it into this version. Do not offer them, even as an aside. If an
owner asks for one of the four dropped formats, say plainly it is not one of
this skill's four, and point at the closest supported alternative (a quiz
about "what's your bottleneck" is usually a guide or a checklist wearing a
different hook).

Every format below ships as one thing: a real, designed PDF, built per
`references/pdf-pipeline.md`. The format only changes the shape of the
content, never the bar for how it looks.

## The shared quality bar: specificity beats volume

Before drafting any format, hold it to this. A lead magnet fails for one
reason almost every time: it is generic enough that it could carry any
business's logo. The fix is never more pages - it is more specifics per
page.

- **Every item, step, or template pulls something real from the brain** -
  the business's own steps (`brain/methodology.md`), its own words for the
  problem (`brain/audience.md`), a number or result it can actually stand
  behind (`brain/proof/`). Generic advice a competitor could have written
  is the failure mode, whatever format it is wearing.
- **Length serves the promise, not a page-count instinct.** A 20-item
  checklist where five items matter and fifteen are filler is worse than an
  honest 8-item one. Cut before you pad.
- **One quick win, clearly delivered.** The reader should be able to use
  something from this in the next ten minutes, not just feel informed.
- **It reads like this business, not like a template with the logo
  swapped in.** If swapping the business name for a competitor's leaves
  every sentence equally true, it is not specific enough yet.

## Checklist

**What it is.** A scannable list of steps or checks, each with just enough
explanation to act on it. The fastest format for the reader to consume and
the fastest to draft well, which makes it the easiest one to get lazy on.

**When it's the right pick.** The business has a real, repeatable process
with distinct steps, and the goal is a fast, low-friction "yes" - a first
touch, not a deep-trust builder. Good for busy, already-problem-aware
audiences who want the answer, not the theory.

**Structure outline:**
- Intro line - what this checklist covers and who it is for, one or two
  sentences.
- 7-15 items, each: the step or check, one line of concrete explanation (not
  just the step restated), and why skipping it costs something specific.
- Closing line - what the reader has when every box is checked, tied to the
  Phase 0 offer path.

**Realistic length.** 3-6 pages including the cover and CTA back page. If it
is running past 15 items, it is probably two checklists or a guide.

**What makes it useful, not a coupon.** Each item has to be a real
instruction someone could act on today, sourced from
`brain/methodology.md`'s actual steps - not a restated headline ("Know your
audience" is not an item; "Pull the last 10 support tickets and circle the
word customers use most for the problem" is). A checklist of restated
headlines is a table of contents pretending to be a deliverable.

## Guide

**What it is.** The deep-dive format: a real explanation of a process,
framework, or topic, in the business's own method and language, not a quick
list. This is where the business's named ideas from
`brain/methodology.md`'s "What we call things" earn their keep.

**When it's the right pick.** The topic has real "why" behind the "what" -
the audience needs to understand a framework or a way of thinking, not just
follow steps. Good for a business whose edge IS the way it explains things,
and for an audience that is problem-aware but not yet sure a solution like
this exists.

**Structure outline:**
- Opening - the problem stated the way the audience actually describes it
  (`brain/audience.md`'s own words), and the promise of what changes by the
  end.
- The method or framework, named - use the business's own name for it if it
  has one; introduce it plainly if not.
- One section per step or pillar of the method: what it is, why it matters,
  a concrete example, the mistake people make here
  (`brain/methodology.md`'s "mistakes we see over and over" is the source
  for this, never invented).
- A short recap that ties the whole method together in a few lines.
- Closing bridge to the Phase 0 offer - what someone does next if they want
  this done for them, or done faster.

**Realistic length.** 6-15 pages including cover and CTA. Long enough to
actually teach something, short enough to finish in one sitting.

**What makes it useful, not a coupon.** The opinions and named ideas from
`methodology.md`'s "what we believe that others do not" are the strongest
material available - an opinion cannot be copied the way a tip can. A guide
that reads like it could have come from any well-written blog post on the
topic has skipped the one thing that makes it this business's guide.

## Template / swipe pack

**What it is.** Ready-to-use material the reader fills in or copies
directly - not advice about what to write, the actual thing to send, post,
or say, with the blanks marked.

**When it's the right pick.** The business's real edge is a document,
message, or script it has refined over time (an email that always converts,
a script that handles an objection, a structure that always works), and the
goal is "save them the blank page," not "teach them a concept."

**Structure outline:**
- Intro - how to use these, and what to swap for their own details.
- Each template: a name for it, the full ready-to-use text with
  `[BRACKETED FILL-IN]` sections marked clearly, and one filled-in example
  showing it in use.
- 3-6 templates. Fewer, sharper templates beat a long thin set.
- Closing tips for adapting the tone to their own voice.

**Realistic length.** 4-10 pages depending on template count and length.

**What makes it useful, not a coupon.** Every template has to be something
the business actually uses or would stand behind sending, not a generic
fill-in-the-blank found in any copywriting roundup. If a template is thin on
brain material, mark it `[PLACEHOLDER: real example needed from the owner]`
rather than inventing a plausible-sounding one - a fabricated "swipe" is the
opposite of the format's whole promise.

## Mini-course outline

**What it is.** A short, paced roadmap - a number of days or steps, each
with one focus and one action - delivered as a single PDF the reader works
through at their own speed. This is NOT a drafted email sequence; if the
owner wants the roadmap actually delivered as a real multi-day email drip,
that is `email-write`'s sequence craft, offered as a follow-on handoff, not
rebuilt here.

**When it's the right pick.** The transformation genuinely needs more than
one sitting - a real change in habit, skill, or setup that happens over
days, not a single insight. Good for a nurture goal: building trust over a
short structured arc rather than delivering one fast win.

**Structure outline:**
- Course intro - the promise, how many days/steps, roughly how long each one
  takes.
- One section per day/step: its single focus, a short teaching point, the
  one action to take before moving on, a checkbox.
- A completion section - what the reader has built or changed by the end,
  and the bridge to the Phase 0 offer for anyone who wants to go further or
  faster.

**Realistic length.** 5-10 pages for a 3-7 day/step arc. More steps than
that stops being a "mini" course.

**What makes it useful, not a coupon.** Each day needs its OWN single
action, not a slice of one big task spread across days to look paced. If
day 3's action does not depend on day 1 and 2 being done, question whether
this needed to be paced at all rather than delivered as a guide.
