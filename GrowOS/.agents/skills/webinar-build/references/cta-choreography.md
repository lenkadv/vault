# CTA choreography — seed early, pitch once

The pitch/transition craft mined alongside `references/webinar-structure.md`.
Read this before drafting the Pitch segment and the Q&A close in
`segment-scripts.md`, and again during the choreography pass (SKILL.md
Phase 7) that checks the finished script and slides outline against it.

**On the examples below.** A line marked "Example" is a genuinely generic
craft line from the mined material — kept only because it names no product,
no price, and no person. It shows the MOVE, never text to paste unchanged
into a customer's script; a real script uses this business's own true
material for the same move. Where the source's real line could not be shown
without naming something specific, only the pattern is described here, with
no illustration at all.

## No-name-before-reveal

The offer is never named, described, or priced before its one dedicated
reveal slide, roughly 70% through the show. Every earlier mention uses a
neutral placeholder ("my setup," "what I built," "the thing I'm about to show
you") — never the real name, never a price, never a feature list specific
enough to identify it.

**The one documented exception: proof keeps its attribution regardless of
timing.** If a real, approved testimonial or a dated proof-of-concept appears
anywhere before the reveal (the Open's "credible proof-of-concept" beat, for
instance), it is still attributed normally — a real customer's real name, if
that's how the `brain/proof/` entry is written — even though the offer itself
hasn't been named yet. Showing a real result is not the same as naming the
product that produced it. Generalized lesson: decide this explicitly, in
writing, for every webinar this skill builds, rather than letting exceptions
to the naming rule creep in ad hoc as the script gets drafted.

## Seed early, pitch once

A single ~15-second "gift tease" plants a reason to stay, early, with zero
description of what's being sold (see `webinar-structure.md`'s Open,
step 3). There is exactly one full pitch arc, near the end. No fake urgency,
no printed countdown inside the offer section, no repeated calls to buy
scattered through the teaching, and a real deadline — once one exists — is
spoken exactly once in the entire show, never printed on more than one
surface (see "Honest urgency," below).

**Six closing archetypes to consciously choose to use or reject, never to
reach for as a default:**

1. A false-humility "just one more thing" callback style.
2. An over-familiar "buddy" close that presumes a closeness the room hasn't
   earned.
3. A shame-based "if you're not serious you should leave now" close.
4. An us-versus-them tribal close.
5. A punitive or scarcity-shaming close ("some of you will regret this").
6. An immediate hard-sell-via-social-proof close (leaning on crowd pressure
   instead of the offer's own merits).

None of these is banned outright — some businesses and some rooms can carry
one deliberately. Treat the list as a checklist to run the draft against, and
require a conscious choice either way, rather than defaulting into one of
these because it's a familiar webinar-copywriting trope.

## The open-loop device

Name a specific, unexplained preference or choice early ("I tried several
approaches, I settled on two — I'll tell you why later"), then explicitly
close the loop later in the show ("Remember I said I'd tell you why? Here's
why."). This manufactures a reason to keep watching without any artificial
urgency, and rewards patient viewers with a real payoff instead of a trick.

**Example** (generic pattern, no product or persona specifics — shows the
move, not text to copy verbatim): *"I tried four other ways of doing this
before I settled on the two I actually use. Why those two? Hang on to that
question — I'll answer it before we're done."*

**Tracking discipline.** The moment an open loop gets written, log it: what
was seeded, and exactly where its payoff lands later in the script. An open
loop that never closes is a scripting bug to fix before the draft moves on,
not a minor stylistic slip (`webinar-structure.md`, Section 5, lesson 11).
The same discipline applies to any audience-supplied input collected in an
engagement beat (`webinar-structure.md`, Section 3, beat 3) — plan its full
callback chain the moment the question is written.

## The money-echo device

A two-line card closing every teaching chapter: one strong reframing
headline, one sub-line, translating the teaching into the ONE shared success
metric from the Open — never naming the product or a price at this point.

**Generic shape:** *"[Outcome achieved]. [Concrete resource given back]."* —
for example, a headline naming what changed, paired with a sub-line naming
the resource returned (time, money, or both). Build this fresh, in this
business's own voice, from what the chapter actually taught — the shape
generalizes; the words do not.

## The permission bridge

Immediately before pivoting to the pitch, state plainly that leaving now is
completely fine — the audience already has the promised free value — paired
with a soft, non-pressuring invitation to stay for those curious how it was
packaged. This is deliberately a trust move, positioned against the six
closing archetypes above, not a rhetorical trick to lower defenses right
before a hard sell.

## Macro urgency right before the ask, not earlier

The single biggest urgency argument in the whole show opens the Pitch, rather
than living inside the teaching — the big, market-level version of Chapter
3's small, personal urgency argument (a widening-gap framing, a "this is now
table stakes" framing). Landing it right before the reveal keeps the last
teaching chapter short and non-salesy, and puts urgency exactly where the ask
is about to happen.

## The offer arc, in order

Once the reveal happens, the rest of the Pitch follows this sequence. Nothing
here is a fixed script — it is the order that held across every real
revision of the mined material:

1. **Re-open urgency at macro scale** (see above).
2. **The reveal.** The offer is named for the first time, paired with a
   concrete first look — an image or screenshot, not only a verbal
   description — so the reveal is visible, not only spoken.
3. **The ownership reframe**, if genuinely true of this offer: "you're not
   buying a snapshot, you're building something that keeps getting better."
   Skip this step entirely for a one-time-use offer where it isn't true —
   never force a compounding-value frame onto something that doesn't compound.
4. **Future-pace a concrete, near-term scenario** — a specific, mundane,
   vivid picture of the offer already working.
5. **Proof wall.** Clustered testimonials (video and text), each attributed
   to a real, named, approved customer — pulled only from `brain/proof/`
   entries marked `approval: approved`. Quote and name get more visual
   weight than body text.
6. **"What's inside."** A capability/category overview. Still no prices.
7. **The bonus stack.** Each bonus stated as a headline plus, explicitly, the
   ONE fear it kills — never a feature description. Reconcile the whole
   stack against the literal, current offer in `brain/business.md` every
   single pass; a bonus that no longer ships does not appear.
8. **Full stack recap.** Read once, top to bottom. No dollar-total stacking.
9. **The "doors" comparison.** The cost of solving the same problem another
   way (an agency, a hire, a patchwork of point-tools, doing it themselves) —
   placed immediately before price so the anchor is still fresh.
10. **Price.** Stated once, plainly, with an explicit acknowledgment that it
    is real money, then a deliberate pause.
11. **Guarantee.** Reframed away from pure risk-reversal into an
    informed-decision frame (see "Honest urgency," below, for the reusable
    reframing line), plus a stated reason the guarantee is safe to offer.
12. **Scripted objection slides.** Each headlined as the audience's own
    thought, in quotes ("you might be thinking: ___"), answered in one clear
    on-slide line plus a fuller spoken script. Promote an objection here only
    once real evidence — past sales calls, support tickets, a pattern from
    `brain/audience.md` — shows it is common, not a guess; leave genuinely
    rare objections for live Q&A instead.
13. **The gut-check question.** One direct "would you actually use it?"
    question, with a mandated multi-second pause before advancing.
14. **A founder's-mission beat.** Why the presenter is doing this at all —
    positioned deliberately late, right before the close, to humanize the
    ask.
15. **Existing-customer acknowledgment.** So already-paying customers in the
    room aren't left out of a pitch aimed at prospects.
16. **The CTA slide.** Visually minimal — the link, the price, one line.
    Every mechanic (deadline, what happens after clicking, urgency framing)
    is SPOKEN, never printed (see "CTA-slide minimalism," below).

## Q&A as scripted pitch extension

Every question is answered as an objection, using agree-amplify-reframe: agree
with the real concern underneath it, amplify why it matters, then reframe
toward the answer. A seed bank of pre-written answers covers the case where
chat goes quiet (or, for a pre-recorded/evergreen format, IS the whole
segment — see `webinar-structure.md`'s Q&A section). Every seeded answer ends
by pointing back at the same single link.

**Two distinct forks, worded differently on purpose:**

- An **identity-based fork** closes the CTA slide itself: "there are two
  kinds of [audience]; you already know which one you are."
- A separate **practical build-vs-buy fork** closes the Q&A segment: "build it
  yourself, or use mine — either way, start."

Keeping the wording distinct avoids the ending feeling like it repeats
itself.

**A near-zero-friction chat beat exactly at the moment of decision.** A "drop
an emoji if you're taking this" beat sits inside the CTA moment itself — the
lowest possible commitment gesture, timed to the instant of decision rather
than before or after it.

## CTA-slide minimalism, mechanics spoken not printed

The CTA slide itself carries only the link, the price, and one line.
Everything about deadline, urgency, and what happens next is spoken aloud,
never printed — deliberately separating what's SAID (can carry warmth and
urgency) from what's SHOWN (stays clean, and stays accurate even on a replay
that outlives the original moment).

## Honest urgency

Real urgency is a fact the owner stated, never an inference and never a
manufactured deadline to create pressure. Where a real deadline or cap
exists, state it plainly, spoken exactly once, late in the show, and never
printed on more than one surface. Where no real deadline exists, close on fit
and the future-pace instead — a soft, honest close beats a fabricated
deadline every time.

Frame urgency as a true tradeoff, never a threat:

**Example** (generic, no product specifics — reusable verbatim in any
honest-urgency framing): *"Doing nothing is also hard, just later and worse.
Pick your hard."* · *"Motivation fades. Systems don't."*

**Example** (guarantee reframed as an informed decision, not risk-reversal —
generic, reusable verbatim regardless of industry): *"I'm not asking you to
decide forever tonight. I'm asking you to make an informed decision — the
only way to really know is from the inside, not from a slide."*

**Example** (the gut-check question — fully generic, works for any offer;
its power is the enforced silence after it, not the wording): *"If this ran
the way I just showed you, would you actually use it?"*

**Example** (a well-known industry idiom, not proprietary to any business —
useful shorthand for outcome-over-feature framing): *"Nobody wants a drill,
they want a hole in the wall."*

**Example** (a common rebuttal-of-scattered-alternatives frame — generic and
reusable against any "but there's a free version of the pieces" objection):
*"A pile of recipes has never once cooked dinner."*

## Objection pre-handles

Before a skeptic can raise the obvious rebuttal, the presenter states it
themselves, in their own words, undercutting it by already having admitted
it. This belongs inside Chapter 2's belief-chain "break it with" step (see
`webinar-structure.md`) as much as it belongs in the Pitch — concede the
skeptic's best point before anyone else raises it, then show what actually
makes the difference.

## Fabrication traps specific to choreography

- **Invented stories.** A connect/credibility beat, a founder's-mission beat,
  or any anecdote used anywhere in the choreography comes only from
  `brain/stories/`, as written. No story invented to fit a beat that needs
  one.
- **Invented student or customer results.** Every number, quote, or result
  on the proof wall or in the credible proof-of-concept beat comes only from
  `brain/proof/`, byte-identical, and only where `approval: approved`. A
  `pending` entry is not cleared to use.
- **Fake scarcity.** No "seats filling up," no invented cap, no manufactured
  countdown. A real cap is a fact the owner stated or one already sitting in
  `brain/business.md`.
- **Invented deadlines.** A real deadline is owner-stated, spoken exactly
  once, never inferred and never manufactured to create pressure. No real
  deadline exists: close evergreen, and say so plainly rather than reaching
  for a soft-sounding fake one.
- **Invented bonuses.** A bonus in the stack is real and actually ships,
  reconciled against `brain/business.md`'s current offer every pass.
