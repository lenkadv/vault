# Arcs

`references/arcs.md` holds the four slide arcs Step 3 picks from: **story**,
**listicle**, **myth-bust**, and **steps**. Each one is a reusable skeleton, not
a script - the same arc can carry a lesson, a launch, or a list of mistakes;
what stays fixed is the shape, not the subject. Pick the arc that matches the
shape of what this business actually has to say, then write the real slide
copy from the brain (SKILL.md Step 2) - never from the bracketed illustrations
below, which exist only to show the shape.

Every arc shares one structure underneath: a hook slide that stands alone, a
run of progression slides that each move exactly one idea forward, and a
landing slide that pays it off. What differs, arc to arc, is HOW the
progression slides earn the next swipe. `references/slide-craft.md` carries
the rules that apply inside every arc, whichever one you pick - word bands,
the one-idea rule, the persistent-motif rule, the honesty beat, readability
floors, platform dimensions, and the sealing checklist. This file is only
about the shape; that one is about how each slide gets built.

---

## 1. Story

**Job:** build connection by accumulating one true scene per slide toward a
reveal.

**When it fits:** there is a real, sequential moment or change to walk through
- an origin, a turning point, a real project from start to finish. Not a fit
when all you actually have is a list of facts with no throughline; that is
`listicle` or `steps` instead.

**Skeleton:**
- **Hook (S1):** the opening scene, told so it stands alone - the situation as
  it stood, specific enough to place the reader in it.
- **Progression (S2 to S(n-1)):** one scene per slide, each one moving the
  story forward - never repeating or summarizing what the last slide already
  said.
- **Landing (S(last)):** the reveal - what changed, plus the honesty beat and
  the CTA.

**Typical length:** 5-8 slides. A story arc usually wants more room than the
others; a reveal that arrives by slide 4 rarely had enough scenes to earn it.

**Pattern notes:**
- One scene per slide, accumulating straight through to the reveal - never
  resetting the reader to an earlier point once a scene has passed.
- The hook scene has to stand alone. Someone who only ever sees slide 1 should
  still get a complete, if smaller, moment - not a fragment that needs slide 2
  to make sense.
- The reveal lands harder when the earlier scenes under-explain rather than
  over-explain. Leave the last slide something real to answer.

**Shape, illustrated:**
> S1: [The situation as it actually stood - specific, dated if that helps.]
> S2 to S(n-1): [One real beat per slide - what happened next, told plainly.]
> S(last): [What changed, stated plainly], plus the one honest limit or
> promise and the next step.

## 2. Listicle

**Job:** one item per slide, each one written so the reader can genuinely
self-identify against it.

**When it fits:** there is a real list - signs, mistakes, options, types -
where every item is a distinct, real thing on its own, not a forced slice of
one bigger idea. Not a fit when the "items" all restate the same point in
different words; that is one idea, not a list.

**Skeleton:**
- **Hook (S1):** names the list and its exact count, and states plainly what
  finding yourself in it means for the reader.
- **Progression (S2 to S(n-1)):** one list item per slide - never two on one
  slide, never one item split across two.
- **Landing (S(last)):** which item matters most, or a reframe that ties the
  list together, plus the honesty beat and the CTA.

**Typical length:** 5-7 slides (hook, 3-5 items, landing). Fix the count
before writing the hook - the hook states the number, so it cannot drift once
slides are underway.

**Pattern notes:**
- One item per slide, always. A listicle that doubles up items to save a
  slide has stopped being a listicle.
- Each item is written TO the reader, not about a category - "you do this"
  reads stronger than "some people do this." The self-identify pull is what
  makes the arc work, not the list itself.
- The count is fixed upfront and the hook states it. If a slide gets cut
  later, the hook's number has to change with it.

**Shape, illustrated:**
> S1: [N] [things] that [the outcome or the warning]. See which ones are you.
> S2 to S(n-1): [One item, named plainly, written so the reader can place
> themselves in it.]
> S(last): [Which one matters most, or the thread that ties them together],
> plus the honest limit or promise and the next step.

## 3. Myth-bust

**Job:** strike a wrong belief the audience actually holds, then land the true
one.

**When it fits:** there is a real, common misconception this audience
actually carries - something heard in real conversations, objections, or
reviews. Not a fit when the "myth" is a strawman invented to have something to
correct; ground it in `brain/audience.md`, not in a convenient argument.

**Skeleton:**
- **Hook (S1):** names the myth plainly, or poses the question the myth is a
  wrong answer to.
- **Progression (S2 to S(n-1)):** one wrong belief or reason struck per
  slide, with the honest correction for that one point.
- **Landing (S(last)):** the true answer lands clearly, plus the honesty beat
  and the CTA.

**Typical length:** 4-6 slides. A myth-bust that takes more than four struck
points usually has more than one myth in it - split it.

**Pattern notes:**
- Strike the wrong answer, land the true one - the visual motif IS the strike
  (see `references/slide-craft.md`'s persistent-motif rule), and it has to
  progress one strike per slide, never all at once on the landing slide.
- Never invent a myth nobody actually holds just to have something to
  correct - it has to trace to a real, common belief in `brain/audience.md` or
  `brain/lessons/`.
- Land the true answer without making the reader feel foolish for having
  believed the myth. The tone stays on the reader's side of the strike, not
  above it.

**Shape, illustrated:**
> S1: [The common belief, stated plainly, or the question it is a wrong
> answer to.]
> S2 to S(n-1): [One wrong reason, struck, with the honest correction for
> that point.]
> S(last): [The true answer, landed clearly], plus the honest limit or
> promise and the next step.

## 4. Steps

**Job:** walk one real process, one step per slide, with a progress motif
that visibly advances.

**When it fits:** there is a real, ordered process - a how-to, a setup, a
framework - where the order matters and skipping a step would actually break
it. Not a fit for a set of independent tips with no real sequence; that is
`listicle`.

**Skeleton:**
- **Hook (S1):** names the whole outcome up front - what the reader will have
  by the last slide.
- **Progression (S2 to S(n-1)):** one step per slide, the progress motif
  advancing by exactly one increment each time.
- **Landing (S(last)):** the completion, the finished state shown plainly,
  plus the honesty beat and the CTA.

**Typical length:** 4-6 slides. Enough steps to be real, few enough that
every one earns its own slide - a step that is really two steps stapled
together should be split, not compressed.

**Pattern notes:**
- A progress motif that advances - a bar filling, a counter ticking, a
  checklist checking off - named once and moved by exactly one step per slide
  (see `references/slide-craft.md`).
- Steps stay in the order they actually happen. A steps arc that reorders for
  drama stops being trustworthy as a how-to.
- The completion slide shows the finished state itself, not just "step 5 of
  5" - the reader should be able to see what they would actually have.

**Shape, illustrated:**
> S1: [The outcome, named plainly - what you will have when this is done.]
> S2 to S(n-1): [Step name.] [What it is, in one line.] Motif: [state, e.g.
> "3 of 5 filled."]
> S(last): [The finished state, shown plainly], plus the honest limit or
> promise and the next step.

---

**One rule for all four:** the persistent motif (`references/slide-craft.md`)
is what makes a set of slides read as one carousel instead of four separate
images posted next to each other. Pick the arc for the shape of the message;
pick the motif for the shape of the swipe.
