# Ad Archetypes

Purpose: a menu of creative starting points for drafting Meta ad concepts. Read this before
generating a round. Pick lenses from here, then build the actual ad in the account's own voice,
using its own proof.

## What these are for

An archetype is a lens for finding ONE concept. It is not a template to fill in. Treat "Beats" as
the shape of an idea, not a script to paste words into. Fill in the brackets and you get an outline
with emotion and polish added on top. Use the archetype to hunt for the actual angle, world, and
hook that fits this business today, and you get an ad.

The whole point of a catalog like this is raw material for diversity. Twenty five different lenses
give twenty five genuinely different starting points. Twenty five filled-in templates just give one
ad wearing different outfits, and an audience (and Meta's own duplicate detection) notices that
faster than most people expect.

## How to pick

Run two checks, in order, before locking a choice for a round.

1. **Match the audience's actual position.** A cold audience that has never heard of the problem
   needs a different opening move than a warm list that already agrees and is just stalling on the
   decision. Every entry below names the awareness stage and offer type it suits, so start there.
2. **Check the account's dna log.** Before you commit to a lens, look at what ran in recent rounds.
   An archetype that already shipped twice this month is not a fresh idea, it is a repeat with new
   words. Reach for something the account has not tried yet, or has not tried in a while.

## The reuse rule

An archetype picked twice within recent rounds needs a genuinely different world, scene, and hook
the second time. Same lens plus same execution twice in a row is not two ads, it is one ad shown
twice with a coat of paint. Change at least one of: who is in it, where it is set, what opens the
ad, or what proof carries it. This is what keeps a round from reading as five versions of one idea,
and it is what keeps Meta from quietly throttling near-duplicates.

## Stop the scroll

These lenses exist to buy the first three seconds. Use them cold, when the job is simply getting a
stranger to stop moving their thumb.

### Cold Open

**What it is:** Start in the middle of the action with zero setup, so the viewer has to keep
watching just to figure out what happened.

**When it wins:** Cold, top-of-funnel audiences scrolling fast. Works for any offer, best when
something visually odd or unexpected is already happening on screen when the ad opens.

**Beats:**
1. Open on the middle of the action, already in motion, no context given.
2. Let the confusion sit for a beat, then drop one line that half-explains it.
3. Reveal what is actually going on and how it connects to the offer.
4. Show the result.
5. Close on the offer with one plain next step.

**Example sketch:** A Willow & Wren ad opens on someone crying happy tears over an open box, no
context. Three seconds in, text reads: "She ordered the wrong size. Here's what happened next."
Cut to the much better story.

**Pitfall:** Being confusing for its own sake. If the half-explanation in beat 2 does not land
fast, the viewer decides it is not worth solving and scrolls on.

### Loud Confession

**What it is:** Open with an admission that sounds like it should not be said out loud: a mistake,
a wrong belief, something got embarrassingly wrong for too long.

**When it wins:** Warm and retargeting audiences who have already heard the polished pitch. Fits
any offer where the founder or a real customer can speak in first person.

**Beats:**
1. State the confession flat, with no excuse attached.
2. Say plainly what that mistake actually cost, in time, money, or trust.
3. Show what changed.
4. Show the fix in action.
5. Invite the viewer to skip the mistake.

**Example sketch:** Summit Books & Tax: "I filed my own taxes for six years to save money. That
decision cost me a lot more than an accountant ever would have." Then the real numbers, then the
fix.

**Pitfall:** A confession that was not actually risky reads as fake humility. If nothing real was
lost, the ad falls flat before beat 2 even lands.

### The Dare

**What it is:** Put a real stake on the table in public: a timed bet with a visible cost if it
fails.

**When it wins:** Cold to warm audiences, offers with a demonstrable process that fits inside a
fixed window (days to a few weeks).

**Beats:**
1. Name the bet and the deadline out loud.
2. Name what happens if it fails: a refund, giving the work away, eating the cost.
3. Show the work happening, day by day or step by step.
4. Hit a real wall or doubt moment. Do not skip the hard part.
5. Land the result against the original bet.

**Example sketch:** The Steady Coach: "I'm giving myself 14 days to take a client from zero
pipeline to 3 booked calls, on camera, or this program is free for everyone watching." Days 1, 6,
and 14 marked as visible checkpoints.

**Pitfall:** A dare with no real downside is just a claim in a costume. The stake has to be
something the viewer believes could genuinely happen.

### Borrowed Eyes

**What it is:** Hand the test to someone other than the founder or the brand, a real customer, a
stranger, a skeptical friend, and film the honest, unscripted reaction.

**When it wins:** Any stage, especially useful once the founder's own voice has gone stale in the
account. Works best for offers a third party can try in real time.

**Beats:**
1. Introduce the person and their starting skepticism.
2. Hand them the product or process with no coaching.
3. Let the camera catch the real, unscripted reaction.
4. Ask them the one question that actually matters.
5. Close on their unprompted verdict.

**Example sketch:** Summit Books & Tax hands a shoebox of receipts to a friend who has never used a
bookkeeper. The camera catches her reaction when the categorized report shows up the next morning.

**Pitfall:** Coaching the reaction. A stiff, obviously scripted "wow" reads worse than no
testimonial at all.

### False Start

**What it is:** Open by breaking the visual pattern the viewer expects, upside down, used the
"wrong" way, an action that looks like a mistake, then correct course.

**When it wins:** Cold audiences scrolling fast. Strongest for physical products and anything
visually demonstrable.

**Beats:**
1. Show the unexpected or "wrong looking" visual first.
2. Hold it a full second longer than feels comfortable.
3. Cut to the explanation that makes it make sense.
4. Show the intended use or result.
5. Close on the offer.

**Example sketch:** A Willow & Wren throw blanket gets used as a picnic mat in the rain. The camera
holds on it looking soaked and ruined, then cuts to: "It's actually built for this." Demo of it
drying instantly.

**Pitfall:** If the "wrong" visual does not connect cleanly to a real product benefit, it reads as
a gimmick with no payoff.

## Build belief

These lenses exist to change what the viewer thinks is true, about the problem, the category, or
themselves, before asking for anything.

### Real Reason

**What it is:** Name the actual, specific cause of the problem, something other than effort or
willpower, and position the offer as the one thing built to fix that exact cause.

**When it wins:** Crowded categories where the buyer has already tried other things and is tired of
being told to just try harder. Mid-funnel, problem-aware to solution-aware. Any offer type.

**Beats:**
1. Name the reason people usually assume is the cause: effort, discipline, or money.
2. Reject it directly.
3. Name the real, more specific cause.
4. Show why that cause explains the past failures.
5. Position the offer as built for that one cause.

**Example sketch:** The Steady Coach: "You don't have a motivation problem. You have a 'nobody
ever showed you the actual steps' problem. That's a completely different fix."

**Pitfall:** A vague "real reason" like mindset is not specific enough to feel like a discovery. It
has to be concrete enough that the viewer recognizes it in their own life immediately.

### Sacred Cow

**What it is:** Say the thing the audience has been told to believe that is actually wrong, then
hand them a plainer rule to replace it.

**When it wins:** Audiences numb to standard claims in a mature category. Best for cold traffic,
and only if the brand is willing to take a side.

**Beats:**
1. State the rule everyone follows without question.
2. Say plainly that it is wrong, or at least incomplete.
3. Give the one piece of evidence that proves it.
4. Offer the replacement rule.
5. Show what following the new rule looks like.

**Example sketch:** Summit Books & Tax: "More spreadsheets don't make your books more accurate.
They just make the mistakes harder to find." Then the simpler system.

**Pitfall:** Attacking a belief the audience does not actually hold. If the sacred cow is not real
to them, the ad reads as arguing with nobody.

### Myth Check

**What it is:** State a specific, widely taught claim, show it is false with real evidence, then
hand over the accurate version.

**When it wins:** Educated, research-minded audiences. Strongest when there is something concrete,
a number, a test, a document, to point to as proof. Any offer.

**Beats:**
1. State the myth in the exact words people usually hear it.
2. Say where it came from or why it spread.
3. Show the evidence that breaks it.
4. State the corrected version plainly.
5. Show what changes once someone acts on the correction.

**Example sketch:** The Steady Coach: "'Post every day or the algorithm buries you.' We pulled the
data on 40 client accounts. Posting frequency was not the variable that moved results. This was."

**Pitfall:** Busting a myth nobody actually believes wastes the whole setup. Confirm the myth is
genuinely common before building an ad around debunking it.

### Subtraction Proof

**What it is:** Prove a real result while naming, out loud, the resource everyone assumes is
required, and stating plainly that it was not there.

**When it wins:** Audiences who believe they are disqualified by something they lack: time, money,
a team, experience. Any offer where the resource gap is genuinely real.

**Beats:**
1. State the result up front.
2. Name the thing people assume made it possible.
3. Say plainly that it was not there.
4. Show what actually replaced it.
5. Make the replacement available to the viewer.

**Example sketch:** Willow & Wren: "We hit our best sales month ever. No ad agency. No in-house
designer. Here's the one thing that actually did the work."

**Pitfall:** If the missing resource was not a real barrier for this audience to begin with, the
subtraction lands as a non-event instead of a revelation.

### Line in the Sand

**What it is:** Name a shared frustration, an old way of doing things, a broken system, a category
of bad actor (never a specific person), and put the brand and the viewer on the same side against
it.

**When it wins:** Categories with a well-known villain, like agencies, gatekeeping middlemen, or
legacy tools. Builds loyalty fast in warm and community-adjacent audiences.

**Beats:**
1. Name the shared frustration plainly.
2. Say what keeps causing it (a system or a behavior, not a named person).
3. Say why it keeps happening.
4. Draw the line: here is our side, here is theirs.
5. Invite the viewer onto the brand's side.

**Example sketch:** Summit Books & Tax: "Tax season shouldn't mean paying a stranger $400 to
explain your own numbers back to you, slower than you'd explain them yourself. We built the
opposite of that."

**Pitfall:** Naming a real competitor or a real person instead of a system turns this into an
attack ad and a compliance risk. Keep the enemy a behavior, never a name.

### I Used to Think

**What it is:** Walk through an old, wrong belief the speaker held, and the exact moment or
evidence that flipped it.

**When it wins:** Anywhere a genuine change of mind exists. Strong for warm audiences who respect a
credible reversal more than a confident claim.

**Beats:**
1. State the old belief in the past tense, no hedging.
2. Show what that belief cost while it was held.
3. Show the moment or evidence that broke it.
4. State the new belief plainly.
5. Show what changed in practice.

**Example sketch:** The Steady Coach: "I used to think clients needed more content from me to get
results. Turns out they needed fewer, better prompts. Here's the actual shift."

**Pitfall:** A reversal that is not a real change of mind, just an old strawman knocked down for
effect, reads as manipulative the moment the viewer notices.

### The Long Ache

**What it is:** The classic three-move build. Name the problem precisely, sit with how it actually
feels day to day, then bring the relief.

**When it wins:** Any offer, any stage. Especially strong when the pain is something the audience
has never seen described accurately before.

**Beats:**
1. Name the problem in one specific sentence, not a vague category.
2. Describe exactly how it shows up day to day, the small moments, not the big abstract cost.
3. Let that discomfort sit for a beat before moving on.
4. Introduce the offer as the direct answer to that specific ache.
5. Show the relief in a concrete, visible way.

**Example sketch:** Summit Books & Tax: "It's not the taxes. It's opening your banking app on a
random Tuesday and having no idea what you're actually looking at." Then: "Here's what it feels
like to just know."

**Pitfall:** Rushing beat 3. If the agitation is one throwaway line, the relief in beat 5 does not
feel earned and the whole ad reads shallow.

## Prove it

These lenses exist to make a claim undeniable. Use them once belief is half-built and the viewer
needs evidence, not more reframing.

### Watch Me Build

**What it is:** Show the real process happening on screen, start to some of the way through, then
hold back the last piece.

**When it wins:** Offers with a process that is genuinely demonstrable on screen. Cold audiences
who trust what they can watch happen more than what they are told.

**Beats:**
1. State what is about to get built and how long it will take.
2. Show the process running, in real steps, with no cuts that hide the work.
3. Hit and solve one real snag along the way.
4. Show the finished result.
5. Name the one part deliberately left out, and where to get it.

**Example sketch:** The Steady Coach screen-records building a full week of client content live, in
one sitting, showing every step but the last two.

**Pitfall:** Editing out every friction point makes the process look fake and unreplicable. One
real snag, solved on camera, does more for belief than a spotless run.

### Open Books

**What it is:** Show real internal numbers on camera, unfiltered, at a level of detail brands don't
usually share.

**When it wins:** Warm and retargeting audiences who have seen the polished pitch and need
something harder to disbelieve. Requires real numbers to show.

**Beats:**
1. Say plainly that this isn't the usual polished update.
2. Show the actual numbers on screen, unedited.
3. Point out the ugly or surprising parts too, not just the wins.
4. Explain what the numbers actually mean.
5. Land on the one number that changes how the viewer reads the whole thing.

**Example sketch:** Willow & Wren opens the actual return-rate dashboard on screen: "Everyone shows
the sales number. Here's the number we don't usually post."

**Pitfall:** Curating away everything uncomfortable defeats the whole premise. If it looks too
clean, the transparency does not register as real.

### The Tracker

**What it is:** Follow one single metric over a set window and reveal what happened to it at the
end.

**When it wins:** Anywhere there is a real number worth tracking. Strong for retargeting audiences
who respond better to evidence than adjectives.

**Beats:**
1. Name the one metric and the tracking window up front.
2. Show the starting point.
3. Check in at a midpoint, honestly, even if it isn't impressive yet.
4. Hold the ending number back one beat longer than feels natural.
5. Reveal it.

**Example sketch:** The Steady Coach tracks one client's booked-calls number for 30 days on camera,
week by week, holding the final number for the last three seconds.

**Pitfall:** Picking a metric that does not actually matter to the viewer. A vanity number tracked
carefully is still a vanity number.

### Two Roads

**What it is:** Put the old way and the new way side by side and let the visual difference make the
case.

**When it wins:** Whenever the improvement is visually or emotionally obvious once both paths sit
side by side. Any stage, any offer.

**Beats:**
1. Set up both paths with a label, nothing else.
2. Show the old way: the cost, the wait, the hassle.
3. Show the new way, same moment, same stakes.
4. Let the two sit on screen together.
5. Name which path leads where by a specific point in time.

**Example sketch:** Split screen, Summit Books & Tax: "Old way" shows a stack of paper and a call
scheduled three weeks out. "New way" shows a photo of a receipt and a categorized report the same
afternoon.

**Pitfall:** Making the old way a strawman that is obviously worse insults the intelligence of
anyone currently living the old way. Keep it recognizable, not cartoonish.

### Their Words

**What it is:** A real customer as the hero of their own story: the struggle, the turning point,
the result, told in their own voice.

**When it wins:** Anywhere a genuine, permissioned customer story with real specifics exists.
Strongest mid to bottom funnel.

**Beats:**
1. Introduce the customer and where they were stuck.
2. Show what they had already tried that did not work.
3. Show the moment the offer entered the picture.
4. Show the specific change, something visible, not just a feeling.
5. Let them say, in their own words, what is different now.

**Example sketch:** A Willow & Wren customer walks through the exact moment her guest room stopped
being a storage dump, using her own phrase for it.

**Pitfall:** Smoothing a real testimonial into brand-speak kills what made it credible. Keep the
customer's actual words, even the awkward ones.

### A Day in This Life

**What it is:** Follow one ordinary day, minute to minute, with the offer woven in naturally
instead of announced.

**When it wins:** Offers that fit into a daily routine. Strongest for building familiarity with
cold audiences who respond better to a scene than a pitch.

**Beats:**
1. Open on a specific, ordinary moment, not a highlight.
2. Move through the day in real time beats, not a highlight reel.
3. Show the offer appear exactly where it naturally would.
4. Show one small, honest friction point handled.
5. Close on the day ending, offer visible but not shouted about.

**Example sketch:** A Steady Coach client's Tuesday: coffee, school drop-off, twenty minutes at the
kitchen table running that day's content prompt before the workday starts.

**Pitfall:** Turning the ordinary day into an obvious commercial with a product shot every ten
seconds. The offer has to earn its place in the scene, not dominate it.

### The Origin

**What it is:** The founder explains, in first person, the real reason the thing got built, usually
a problem they lived through themselves before anyone else needed the answer.

**When it wins:** Any stage where the founder's face and voice carry real weight. Especially strong
for building trust cold.

**Beats:**
1. Set the scene: what was actually happening before this existed.
2. Name the specific moment the gap became obvious.
3. Show what was tried and did not work.
4. Show what got built instead, and why it was built that specific way.
5. Connect it back to the viewer's own version of the same problem.

**Example sketch:** Summit Books & Tax's founder: "I was doing my own client's books by hand at
midnight for the third night running when I finally built the system I actually needed."

**Pitfall:** A founder story with no specific, lived detail reads as generic bio copy. The scene has
to be concrete enough that it could not belong to anyone else.

## Push decision

These lenses exist for the moment belief already exists and the job is closing the gap between
agreeing and acting.

### The Small Number

**What it is:** Set the price next to the real cost of the alternative, the status quo, or the
competitor, so the price looks small by comparison.

**When it wins:** Bottom-of-funnel, price-hesitant audiences. Requires a genuinely comparable
alternative cost.

**Beats:**
1. State the price plainly, with nothing hidden.
2. State what the realistic alternative actually costs, in money, time, or both.
3. Put the two numbers next to each other.
4. Name what the smaller number actually includes.
5. Close on the comparison, not on a discount.

**Example sketch:** Summit Books & Tax: "$60 a month. Or three hours every weekend doing it
yourself, plus whatever the mistakes cost you at filing time."

**Pitfall:** Comparing against a fake or inflated alternative cost breaks trust the moment a sharp
viewer checks the math. Use a number that holds up.

### The Toll

**What it is:** Frame the cost of waiting forward, not as a lecture about the past, but as a plain
look at where six more months of nothing changing actually leads.

**When it wins:** Warm audiences who agree with the problem but keep stalling on the decision. Works
best paired with a specific, visible timeframe.

**Beats:**
1. Name the exact decision the viewer keeps putting off.
2. Walk forward, plainly, to where that path ends in six months if nothing changes.
3. Make that future specific, not vague dread.
4. Contrast it with where acting now leads by that same date.
5. Close on the decision, not on guilt.

**Example sketch:** The Steady Coach: "Six months from now you'll either have a list of 500 people
who trust you, or six more months of 'I'll start when things calm down.' Pick the date, not the
excuse."

**Pitfall:** Tipping into guilt or fear instead of a plain forward look. The tone has to stay level,
a mirror held up, not a scolding.

### Future You

**What it is:** Show the viewer's future self already living the result, shot from their own point
of view, with the offer as the quiet bridge that got them there.

**When it wins:** Top-of-funnel identity and desire building. Needs real footage of the actual
outcome, not stock lifestyle imagery.

**Beats:**
1. Open on the future moment, already arrived, no explanation yet.
2. Let the viewer sit in that feeling for a beat.
3. Name what is different about this version of them.
4. Trace it back, briefly, to the one decision that started it.
5. Hand them that same decision.

**Example sketch:** A Willow & Wren ad opens on someone curled up in a finished, calm living room on
a random Tuesday night. "This isn't a redesign. It's just what Tuesdays look like now."

**Pitfall:** Leaning on generic lifestyle stock instead of a real, specific outcome. If the future
could belong to any brand, it will not stick to this one.

### Get In Early

**What it is:** Name a shift already starting to happen in the category, and frame acting now as
getting ahead of it instead of catching up later.

**When it wins:** Categories genuinely in motion: a new tool, a new platform behavior, a real
trend. Launches and repositioning moments.

**Beats:**
1. Name the shift plainly, with a real, checkable reason it is happening.
2. Show early evidence it is already underway, not a prediction pulled from nowhere.
3. Name what happens to the people who wait until it is obvious.
4. Name what happens to the people who move now.
5. Offer the on-ramp.

**Example sketch:** The Steady Coach: "The creators getting booked right now aren't the ones with
the biggest following. They're the ones who switched to this format first. Here's what that switch
looks like."

**Pitfall:** Predicting a shift with no real evidence behind it turns into hype the moment someone
asks who says so. Ground it in something checkable.

### Count Me In

**What it is:** Sell the belief and the group before the product. Invite the viewer to a side they
want to belong to.

**When it wins:** Brand-building and launch moments. Strongest for audiences who care about
identity and community, not just outcome.

**Beats:**
1. State what the brand believes, plainly, in one sentence.
2. Name who that belief is for.
3. Show what belonging to that group looks like in practice.
4. Name the offer as the door into the group, not the point of the ad.
5. Invite the viewer in directly.

**Example sketch:** Summit Books & Tax: "We think small business owners deserve to actually
understand their own numbers, not just sign where they're told to. If that's you, you're already
one of us."

**Pitfall:** Leading with the product instead of the belief turns this into a normal ad wearing
movement language. The belief has to come first and mean something on its own.

### Two Kinds of People

**What it is:** Split the audience into two visible types and let the viewer self-select which one
they are.

**When it wins:** Fast identity buy-in, especially as a static or carousel format. Any stage,
strongest cold and mid-funnel.

**Beats:**
1. Name type one, specific and recognizable.
2. Name type two, specific and recognizable, clearly the better position.
3. Show the visible marker of each: what they do, own, or believe.
4. Let the contrast make the case with no extra argument.
5. Point to the offer as the door from one side to the other.

**Example sketch:** Split card, Willow & Wren: "People with a junk drawer that's actually four
junk drawers" next to "People with one drawer, and they know what's in it."

**Pitfall:** Making type one an insult instead of a recognizable, forgivable state. The viewer has
to be able to laugh at themselves, not feel mocked.
