# Playbook: sharpen an offer that already exists

Use this when there is a real offer and something about it is not landing. Run it
inside the method in `SKILL.md`, which holds the law every mode obeys.

The whole discipline of this mode is restraint. An offer that is mostly working
does not need rebuilding; it needs one thing changed and then watched. A rebuild
throws away the parts that were fine and leaves you unable to tell what the change
did.

Every business, name and number in this file is made up to show the shape of a good
answer. None of it is a fact about anyone.

## Step 1: name the binding weakness

Read the same brain files as a shape round (`SKILL.md` and `playbooks/shape.md`
list them), plus the offer as it stands today in `brain/business.md`. Then walk the
eight levers and decide which one is the weakest. The weakest one is the
constraint. That is the one you change.

The levers, in the order they govern each other:

1. **The promise** — buyer, outcome, boundary.
2. **Who it is for, and who it is not for.**
3. **What is included, and the job each piece does.**
4. **The differentiating component** the buyer can decide on.
5. **The proof behind the claims.**
6. **The guarantee and the risk reversal.**
7. **The packaging shape** — one thing, core plus add-ons, tiers, separate
   packages, pay per unit.
8. **The price and how it is presented.**

You cannot fix a lever with a broken one above it. New packaging will not rescue a
promise nobody understands. A lower price will not rescue an offer aimed at the
wrong people.

### Read the symptom back to the lever

Start from what the owner reports and what `work/`, `proof/` and `decisions.md`
actually show. Take the first row that matches, working down.

| Symptom | Look here first | What would confirm it |
|---|---|---|
| "They say it sounds interesting, then go quiet" | The promise: no outcome anyone can picture, or no boundary | Ask three recent non-buyers what they thought they were buying |
| "The wrong people keep signing up" | Who it is for, and the missing not-for line | List who stuck and who was hard to serve; the pattern names the group |
| "They always ask what makes us different" | The differentiating component | Read `methodology.md` against `competitors.md` and see if anything is genuinely ours |
| "They want it but stall at the last step" | The risk reversal, aimed at the wrong fear | Find the exact objection in `audience.md` or `brain/inbox/` |
| "Half of what we include never gets used" | What is included; pieces with no job | Ask which parts customers actually take up |
| "A buyer needs two things from the top tier, so we push them up and then drop the price" | The tiers were built from a feature list | Run the tier test: one sentence per tier naming who it is for and what problem it solves |
| "We discount nearly every deal to close it" | Packaging, then positioning — not the number | Count the last ten quotes: how many were discounted, and for what stated reason |
| "Nobody ever pushes back on the price" | The price | Switch to `playbooks/pricing.md` and run the raise signals |
| "They line us up against a cheaper quote and pick the cheaper one" | The unit charged on, and the missing components | `playbooks/pricing.md` for the unit; this playbook for the components |

If two levers are genuinely broken, change the one that sits higher on the list and
say plainly that the other is next round, with what to watch in the meantime. Do
not change both.

If the record does not support any of the rows, say so, and name what would settle
it — usually three conversations with people who did not buy.

## Step 2: change one lever, and only that

Write the round as three things:

- **The binding weakness**, with the record it rests on.
- **The one change**, written as shippable wording.
- **What to watch**, and when to look again — one observable thing the owner can
  see without a dashboard.

Then write, explicitly, what you left alone and why. "The promise and the guarantee
are unchanged; nothing in the record says they are the problem." That sentence is
what makes a sharpen round auditable at day thirty. It is also what stops the
document from reading like a rewrite.

**GOOD.** "The weak lever is who this is for. Your last eleven jobs split into two
groups that want different things, and the current page speaks to neither. The one
change is the who-for and who-not-for lines, in the wording below. Watch the next
ten enquiries: how many arrive already knowing which group they are in. Everything
else — price, guarantee, what is included — is unchanged on purpose."

**BAD.** "Here is a refreshed offer: a new promise line, three tiers instead of
two, a stronger guarantee, a bonus onboarding call and a 15% launch discount." Five
changes at once, so at day thirty nobody can say which one moved anything, and two
of them created obligations the owner never agreed to.

## Step 3: read a discount pattern as a diagnosis

A discount you have to give to close is not a pricing decision. It is the only
lever a seller has left when the packaging is wrong.

Check the last handful of quotes or deals the record holds:

- **What share ended up discounted?** If it is nearly all of them, the number on
  the page is not the real price, and the honest first move is fixing what is being
  sold, not the number.
- **What reason was given each time?** A discount given for a reason nobody decided
  in advance is a leak. A discount given because the buyer needed one piece from a
  higher tier is the tier test failing.
- **Do the discounts cluster in one buyer type?** That is usually two different
  buyers being sold one package.

Decide the three to five things the business will discount for, and the limit on
each, before you touch anything else. Volume, buying more than one thing, and a
longer commitment are the common three. Anything outside the list is a no.

If the brain has no record of past quotes or discounts, say so, and ask the owner
for the last ten from memory — recall of things they witnessed is legitimate
evidence, recorded as their own statement with today's date.

## Step 4: retire what the constraint no longer needs

Offer elements are load-bearing for a stage, not forever. Every piece that exists
to remove an obstacle should name the condition under which it stops being needed —
and a sharpen round is where you check whether that condition has already arrived.

Walk each element and ask: what obstacle does this remove, and is that obstacle
still there? A free first visit added when nobody had heard of the business may be
pure cost now that there is a shelf of approved proof. A long guarantee added to
calm a fear that customers no longer voice is money left in a drawer.

Retiring an element is a legitimate one-lever change. Say what it was there for,
say what replaced it, and say what you will watch in case you were wrong.

## Step 5: an offer that has already failed once

Diagnose before you rebuild or abandon. The two readings are "the offer was wrong"
and "the market will not pay", and they lead to opposite actions, so do not guess.

Check in this order, from the record and the owner's own account:

1. **Did anyone actually see it?** How many times was it put in front of a real
   buyer? If the answer is small, nothing has been tested yet and there is no
   result to read.
2. **How was it presented?** Offered by default, or mentioned only when someone
   asked?
3. **What did the people who said no actually say?** Anything in `inbox/`,
   `audience.md`, or the owner's recollection.
4. **Was anything else different at the time** — a season, a price change, a
   different person selling it?

Only when the offer was genuinely seen, presented properly and still refused does
"the market will not pay" become a defensible read. Until then the default reading
is that the offer was wrong, and that is good news, because an offer is something
you can change.

## Step 6: write the document

One file, `work/strategy/<slug>.md`, `type: offer`, born a draft, as `SKILL.md`
specifies. A sharpen document reads diagnosis-first: the binding weakness, the
record behind it, the one change as shippable wording, what to watch, and then the
list of what was deliberately left alone. It is usually shorter than a shape
document. That is correct, not lazy.

Carry forward every part of the existing offer you did not change, word for word,
so `brain/business.md` can be updated in one clean replacement and nothing is lost
by accident.

## The pass bar, before the Editor gate

The full offer bar in `playbooks/shape.md` still applies to the finished document,
because the whole offer ends up in `brain/business.md`. Write it out line by line
with a pass or a fail against each line, in the work item or in your reply, before
the Editor gate. Three lines are added for a sharpen round:

1. The document names one binding weakness and the specific record it rests on — a
   named brain file, the owner's own words this session, or arithmetic whose inputs
   are named. **Or** it says plainly that the record does not settle it, and names
   what would.
2. Exactly one lever changed. Everything else is listed as unchanged, on purpose.
3. One observable thing to watch is named, with when to look again. Not a
   dashboard. Something the owner can see.

Fix anything that fails before you invoke the reviewer.
