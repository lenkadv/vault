# Example: a plan for a business with more demand than it can serve

Invented business, invented facts: Example Clay Studio, a two-person pottery studio
selling mugs and bowls direct and through independent shops. The fake name sits
inside the sentences on purpose. **No sentence from this page may ever appear in a
real plan** — if one does, the fake name travels with it and the mistake is
obvious. Copy the standard, never the words.

Situation: **capacity constrained.** They already sell out, so adding demand would
only make more people disappointed.

Arc: **the decision list** — numbered decisions, each carrying its own reason, its
doer, and the condition that would reverse it. Chosen because Example Clay Studio
can fire 60 pieces a week and no more, which means every line on the page is that
one number deciding something, and a list of decisions shows that better than a
page of sections would. There is no refusals section, because here a refusal *is* a
decision. There is no separate metrics section either, because each decision names
what would reverse it.

The decision-list arc decides the ending too: this page stops inside the decisions,
because the last decision is simply the last one. There is no closing block of
not-doing, then numbers, then a checkpoint. Compare it with
`plan-starting-from-zero.md`, which uses the chain arc and finishes on the step
that has not started. The two pages share no headings, no running order, and no
ending. That is the point: the arc is chosen from the business, so two businesses
do not get the same document.

This is what would be written into `brain/plan.md`. It runs to about 480 words.

---

# The plan — Example Clay Studio, to 31 December

Example Clay Studio can fire 60 pieces a week and no more. Every decision below is
that number doing the deciding, and they all serve one outcome: six shops that have
re-ordered at least once by 31 December, counted from repeat invoices in the
accounts file. Today: two shops, one of which has re-ordered.

**1. Example Clay Studio sells to independent shops, not to more individuals.**
Why: the last two online drops sold out in under an hour, and three shops asked to
stock us and were turned away (enquiry emails, August). One shop order is 60 pieces
and one afternoon of packing; the Saturday craft market is two days for about 25
pieces (takings sheet; the two shop invoices).
Not for: chains — Example Clay Studio could not fire that volume and would have to
say no. Not wedding-list sites; the two we tried wanted stock levels we cannot
promise (order history, spring).
Reverses if: pieces are left over at the end of a firing cycle (stock sheet).

**2. The Saturday craft market stops after 12 October.** It is the two days a week
decision 1 needs. Doer: the owner tells the market office by 30 September.
Reverses if: fewer than four shops have re-ordered by 31 October (accounts file).

**3. Selling is the owner's half-day. The second potter never leaves the wheel.**
One shop visit a week with samples, plus one email a month carrying the firing
dates — both the owner's, both inside the half-day. The second potter's whole week
stays on the 60 pieces, because decision 1 fails the moment a firing slips.

**4. Nothing new gets made until the mug and the small bowl are always in stock.**
Every new shape is a firing Example Clay Studio does not have.

**5. What Example Clay Studio says stays the same everywhere:** "every piece is
thrown and glazed by the two of us, in firings of 60, and we will never sell
through a chain." All three makers in `competitors.md` list a national retailer on
their own sites, so none of them could say the second half.

**6. 14 October is a question, not a review.** By then Example Clay Studio will know
one thing it does not know today: whether a shop will re-order from the emailed
firing dates alone, with no visit. Three shops get the email and no visit before
that date. If they order, the visit is not what sells and decision 3 shrinks. If
they do not, the visit is the product, and decision 2 bought the two days it needs.

## What this example shows

- A different arc produces a genuinely different page: numbered decisions, no
  refusals section, no metrics section, and the outcome stated once at the top as
  the thing every decision serves.
- **The ending belongs to the arc.** The page stops inside the decisions, with no
  closing sequence at all.
- Each decision carries the arithmetic that justifies it, from named sources.
- "Reverses if" does the work a watched-numbers section would do, and ties each
  number to the specific decision it would overturn.
- The checkpoint is **a question the date must answer**, not a stop rule and not a
  reallocation. Compare it with the stop rule in `plan-starting-from-zero.md`: the
  logic is chosen from the situation, and a business that already knows what it
  needs to know would get a third kind again.
- **One refusal, folded into a decision** (decision 4). The other page carries two.
  Neither count is a target; each is what the folder supported.
- The capacity note is an **allocation**, because this business has two people:
  who does what, rather than a total against an hours budget.
- The positioning line was run against every competitor on file, and the result of
  that test is written down.
- Who it is for comes with who it is not for, each with a reason.
- The missing fact uses the exact `[PLACEHOLDER: ...]` token, in three parts: what
  is missing, why it matters here, and what would settle it.
