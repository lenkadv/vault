# Intervention Library

Each diagnosis maps to ONE primary intervention. The skill prescribes the matched one — no menu. If the prescription doesn't land and the user comes back stuck, the fallbacks listed under each entry are the next moves.

The principle behind these: matched interventions land. Generic advice ("just start!") doesn't, because procrastination has different roots and demands different responses.

---

## Overwhelm — task feels too big

**Primary intervention:** Shrink it. Find the smallest physical action that counts as "starting." Open the doc. Write the title and one sentence. Read the first paragraph of the reference material.

**Prescription template:**
> "Open [thing]. Spend 5 minutes — just 5 — on the smallest possible piece. Don't try to finish anything, just put hands on it. Timer for 5 minutes."

**Why it works:** Overwhelm is your brain pattern-matching the whole task against your current state. Shrinking the unit of work breaks the pattern match. After 5 minutes of touching the work, "the whole task" feels different than it did before.

**Fallback if primary doesn't land:** Even smaller. "Just open the file. That's the entire goal. No other commitment."

---

## Ambiguity — I don't know what to do first

**Primary intervention:** Spend 5 minutes writing down what "done" looks like, then what the immediate-next-concrete-action is. No work on the task yet — just defining it.

**Prescription template:**
> "Don't work on the task. Spend 5 minutes answering two questions on paper: (1) What does 'done' look like? (2) What's the very next concrete action — something you could do in under 15 minutes? When you have an answer to #2, that's your start."

**Why it works:** Ambiguity isn't laziness — it's a planning gap. You can't act on a fog. Forcing the clarity is the unblock.

**Fallback:** Ask the user to explain the task as if to a smart 12-year-old. The act of explaining surfaces the concrete next step.

---

## Fear / perfectionism — afraid it'll be bad, afraid of judgment

**Primary intervention:** Deliberately bad first version. Set a 15-minute timer. Make the worst possible version of the deliverable. Title it "shitty first draft" if it's a doc.

**Prescription template:**
> "Set a timer for 15 minutes. Make the deliberately-bad version of [thing]. The goal is bad. If it comes out good, you've failed the assignment. After 15 minutes, you have something to react to — and reacting is way easier than starting."

**Why it works:** Perfectionism freezes you because the gap between "blank page" and "good version" is too wide. The shitty-first-draft trick replaces "blank page → good" with "blank page → bad → less bad → fine." Editing is easier than creating; this hack shifts the work from creation to editing.

**Fallback:** Lower the bar even further. "Just sketch the outline as 5 bullet points. Don't write sentences."

---

## Boredom / aversion — I just don't want to

**Primary intervention:** Pair with energy. Do the task alongside something that makes it tolerable. Music, a walk-and-talk into a voice memo, a coffee shop, body doubling (someone else working in the same room).

**Prescription template:**
> "Don't fight the boredom — pair around it. Pick one: (a) headphones + a playlist you love, (b) take a 10-min walk first, (c) move to a coffee shop or different room, (d) call someone and tell them you're going to do this on the line. Start the task in the new context. Timer for 25 minutes."

**Why it works:** Boredom-driven procrastination is a state problem, not a will problem. You can't muscle through it; you change the conditions. The task hasn't changed but your relationship to it has.

**Fallback:** If the task is genuinely tedious AND not high-leverage, ask the user: "Does this need to be done by you? Can it be delegated, deferred, or dropped?"

---

## Low energy — depleted, can't focus

**Primary intervention:** Body check first, then permission to defer.

**Prescription template (if it's daytime):**
> "Quick check: water, food, 10-min walk outside. In that order. Then come back to the task with a 25-minute timer. Don't try to push through — that's how the rest of the day burns."

**Prescription template (if after 6pm):**
> "Best move tonight is probably not finishing this. Sleep on it. Tomorrow morning, start with 5 minutes when you're fresher. Permission granted."

**Why it works:** Cognitive work needs cognitive resources. Trying to push through low energy produces bad work AND burns out the user faster. Sometimes the unblock is "do it later when you have the gas in the tank."

**Fallback:** If the deadline is real and TODAY: lower the bar of what "done" means, and aim for the minimum viable version.

---

## No stakes — doesn't matter enough to push through

**Primary intervention:** Clarify the stakes. If they're real, name them concretely. If they're not real, give permission to drop.

**Prescription template:**
> "Two questions, 30 seconds each: (1) What happens if this never gets done? Really — what's the consequence? (2) What happens if it does get done — what becomes possible? If the answers don't move you, the right call might be to drop it. If they do, the stakes are now in your head, which is half the battle."

**Why it works:** Tasks without clear stakes get deprioritized by your brain's reward system, which is doing its job. Either the stakes are real and need surfacing, or they're imaginary and the task should leave your list.

**Fallback:** Direct: "Take it off the list. It's been carrying weight for free."

---

## When the diagnosis doesn't fit cleanly

Sometimes the user's answer doesn't map to one of the six. Read `diagnostic-clues.md` for translation patterns. When in doubt, default to the **ambiguity intervention** — defining "done" and the next concrete action is rarely wrong even if the actual problem is something else.
