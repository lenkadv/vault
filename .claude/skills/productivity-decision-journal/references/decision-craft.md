# Decision Craft — Capturing Decisions Well

Most decision journal entries are weak because the user rushes through them. This file is how to keep the craft tight without being annoying.

## The five most common failures

### 1. Vague problem statement

**Bad:** "Figuring out the pricing thing."

**Good:** "Should I move to annual-only pricing for Acme-tier customers, or keep monthly as a fallback option?"

The problem statement is a question. It has specific subject and specific alternatives. If the user can't state it sharply, the decision isn't ready yet — they're still in problem-shaping, not deciding.

If the user gives a vague problem statement, gently push:
> "Frame it as a question with two or more specific options. What's the choice you're actually making?"

### 2. Single option masquerading as a decision

**Bad:** Options considered: "Going with monthly pricing."

**Good:** Options considered: 
- Annual-only ($X/year)
- Monthly + annual options
- Tiered (Starter / Pro / Acme)

A single "option" isn't a decision — it's an action plan. The journal entry is for cases where the user is choosing among alternatives. If they only have one option, ask:

> "What are you NOT going to do? Even weak alternatives are useful — they show what you're choosing against."

Usually the user has a rejected alternative they hadn't named explicitly.

### 3. Empty "what would change my mind"

**Bad:** "Anything reasonable would change my mind."

**Good:** 
- "If Acme doesn't sign within 30 days at the new pricing, I rethink."
- "If two other accounts churn over the structure change, I revert."
- "If my pricing consultant pushes back hard with specific data, I delay the rollout."

The "what would change my mind" section is the single biggest predictor of decision quality. Decisions you'd never reverse aren't really decisions — they're commitments.

If the user can't name specific signals, ask:
> "Imagine a future where this turns out badly. What evidence would you see in the world?"

The answer is the kill criteria.

### 4. Overconfident likelihoods

**Bad:** Best case 80%, Most likely 90%, Worst case 5%. (Total > 100% and reflects no uncertainty.)

**Good:** Best case 25%, Most likely 50%, Worst case 25%. (Doesn't have to total exactly 100%, but reflects real uncertainty.)

The numbers don't need to be precise — what matters is whether they reflect honest uncertainty. If a user assigns 90% to "most likely", ask:

> "What would make you only 50% confident? Try to imagine what could go wrong."

Often the answer reveals risks they hadn't articulated.

### 5. Anticipated outcomes that are too narrow

**Bad:** Best case "we hit our numbers." Worst case "we miss our numbers."

**Good:** Best case "we hit $X in new ARR and gain a flagship reference customer (Acme). Worst case "we lose 1-2 Acme-tier prospects who wanted monthly flexibility, costing us $Y for the quarter."

Outcome predictions should be SPECIFIC enough that the user can compare to reality later. "We hit our numbers" can't be evaluated on review — what numbers, when, by how much?

## When to push back vs. when to accept

The user is in charge. If they're being lazy on the entry, push. If they're being thoughtful but pragmatic ("I don't know the answer to that yet"), accept and move on. Push when:

- The problem statement is vague enough that you can't tell what they're actually deciding.
- They list one "option."
- They have empty kill criteria.
- Their likelihoods sum to wildly more than 100%.

Don't push when:

- They acknowledge a gap ("I don't have data for that yet — just gut feel").
- They're making a small decision and want a lightweight entry.
- They genuinely can't articulate a section right now.

## Length guidance per entry

- **Light entry** (small decisions): 200-400 words across all sections. Some sections may be 1-2 sentences.
- **Standard entry**: 400-700 words. Each section has 2-4 sentences.
- **Heavy entry** (big bets, complex decisions): 700-1500 words. Worth the time for decisions of real consequence.

Most entries should be in the standard range. Heavy entries are for the few decisions per year that genuinely warrant them.

## The "future me" test

When the user is writing an entry, the test is: would future-you, reading this in 3 months without context, understand what was decided and why?

If a section reads like inside-baseball that depends on remembering today's context, push for one more sentence of explanation. Future-you isn't carrying today's memory.

## What this skill does NOT do

It does not:
- **Tell the user what to decide.** That's not the skill's job.
- **Force structured probability estimates.** Likelihoods can be qualitative ("low/medium/high") if the user prefers.
- **Demand long entries.** Lightweight is fine for small decisions.
- **Override the user's framing.** If the user wants to call something "the Acme deal," that's the name.

The skill is a structured pencil. The user is the thinker.
