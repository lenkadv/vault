# Review Craft — Separating Decision Quality from Outcome Quality

The single most important concept in decision review: **a good decision can have a bad outcome, and a bad decision can have a good outcome.** Both happen because the world is uncertain.

Reviews that fail to separate these produce the wrong lessons.

## Outcome bias — the failure mode

Outcome bias is judging a decision by how it turned out. Examples:

- Drove drunk, got home safely. Outcome: good. Decision: bad.
- Bought a stock that 10x'd because of news no one could have predicted. Outcome: good. Decision: possibly fine, possibly bad — depends on the analysis at the time.
- Hired a candidate who didn't work out. Outcome: bad. Decision: could have been excellent given what was known at the time.

If reviews collapse decision quality into outcome quality, the user "learns" from luck. They become more conservative after bad-outcome-good-decisions, or more reckless after good-outcome-bad-decisions. Either way, they get worse at deciding.

The review's job is to separate the two questions.

## The two-question structure

Every review answers two questions:

1. **What was the OUTCOME?** Specific. Compare to predictions.
2. **Was the DECISION good?** Given what you knew at the time — not what you know now.

The order matters. Capture the outcome first, then deliberately step back into the decision-time mindset to evaluate the decision separately.

## How to evaluate decision quality

Ask the user, in this order:

### Q1: "Given specifically what you knew on [decision date], was this still the right call?"

The phrase "given specifically what you knew" is doing the work. It forces the user to mentally rewind. Variations of the same question:

- "Setting aside what you know now, was the call right at the time?"
- "If you'd had a sibling making the same decision with the same information, would you have advised them differently?"
- "Was your reasoning at the time sound?"

### Q2: "Were any of the kill criteria triggered? Did you adjust?"

This is where the journal pays off. The original entry has a "what would change my mind" section. Did any of those signals appear in the world? If yes, did the user respond — or did they stay committed past the signal?

If the user stuck with a decision past their own kill criteria, that's a process failure independent of outcome. (Common pattern: kill criteria appeared, user rationalized them away.)

### Q3: "Did you misjudge any specific risk or upside?"

Look at the anticipated outcomes from the original entry. Where did the user's expectations diverge from reality?

- **Outcome better than expected** can mean: I was too conservative, or I got lucky.
- **Outcome worse than expected** can mean: I missed a risk I should have seen, or I got unlucky.
- **Outcome about as expected** means: my model was decent — good or bad.

The question is *which*. Usually requires honest reflection, not just answer-by-feel.

### Q4: "What would you do differently NOW, with hindsight?"

This question is fine to ask, but flag it: hindsight bias is real. Just because you know how it turned out doesn't mean you could have predicted it.

The honest version: "Given the information available at decision time, what additional question should I have asked?" That's a process improvement, not just regret.

## Generating the lesson

The lesson section is the long-term value. It's where the user pulls a portable insight out of the review.

Good lessons:
- "I tend to underestimate sales cycle length on new product categories. Next time, multiply my gut estimate by 1.5x."
- "When I'm tired, I default to the loudest stakeholder's preference. Watch for this in late-day decisions."
- "My kill criteria were specific but I didn't actually monitor them. Build a check-in cadence into future big decisions."

Bad lessons:
- "Pricing decisions are hard." (Useless.)
- "I should have done more research." (Vague.)
- "Trust my gut." (Too specific to the outcome, not portable.)

The skill should push gently for portability:
> "How does this lesson apply to a different decision you might face in 6 months?"

If the user can answer that, the lesson is portable. If they can't, the lesson is just situation-specific.

## When the answer is "the decision was just bad"

Sometimes the honest review reveals that the decision was wrong even given what was known at the time. Common patterns:

- The user ignored a signal they'd already noted.
- The user committed before considering alternatives properly.
- The user let a non-decision-related factor drive (fatigue, social pressure, ego).
- The user used a framework that was wrong for the situation.

If the user identifies any of these, capture it directly. The point of the journal isn't to feel good about past decisions — it's to get better at future ones.

## When the answer is "the decision was good but the outcome was bad"

This is the most common revealing pattern, and the one that produces the most growth.

A bad-outcome-good-decision review should NOT lead to "next time I'll be more cautious about [type of decision]." That's overcorrecting for luck.

It should lead to: "this type of decision involves real uncertainty that I correctly modeled. My priors were right. The dice just landed badly. I would make the same call again with the same information."

Help the user resist outcome-bias overcorrection.

## Honesty as the unlock

Reviews only work if the user is honest with themselves. The skill's role is to ask the questions in a way that makes honesty easier:

- Specific questions beat vague ones.
- Frame as data, not judgment.
- Don't reward "I was right." Don't punish "I was wrong." Both are bad framing.
- Frame the outcome as information about the decision, not as the verdict on it.

A user who consistently writes honest reviews — including admitting bad decisions — gets better at deciding over time. A user who uses reviews to feel good about past choices doesn't.
