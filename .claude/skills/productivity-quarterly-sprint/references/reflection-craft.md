# Reflection Craft — Quality Bar for Session 2

Session 2 is the deepest part of the sprint. It's also the one most likely to fail — most quarterly "reflection" is corporate ritual (what worked / what didn't / what we'll do next) that produces nothing the user wouldn't have said off the cuff.

This file is how to make reflection actually generative.

## The shape of good reflection

Good reflection moves the user from facts to meaning. The look-back (Session 1) gives them the facts. Reflection asks: **what does this teach me?**

Reflective answers should be:

- **Specific** — about real things that happened, not generalities
- **Honest** — including the unflattering parts
- **Generative** — produces a lesson or insight, not just a summary
- **In the user's own voice** — not sanitized, not corporate-speak

## Per-question guidance

### Q1: What did I accomplish this quarter that I'm proud of?

**The trap:** listing accomplishments as if for a resume.

**The aim:** specific moments where the user can identify what was hard about it and what they did to overcome that hard part.

Push for:
- "I shipped X" → "What was hard about shipping X, and what did you do well?"
- Generic pride → "What specifically about it made you proud?"

The pride comes from the difficulty, not the outcome. A hard thing done well is the answer.

### Q2: What did I plan but not do? What does that pattern teach me?

**The trap:** "I was too busy."

**The aim:** patterns. The pattern is the answer.

Push for:
- "I didn't get to X" → "What was X, and what kept happening when you tried?"
- Single misses → "Across the things you didn't do, is there a pattern? Type of work? Time of day? Relationship?"

The pattern reveals something true about the user's actual priorities (revealed by behavior) vs. stated priorities (revealed by plans).

### Q3: What surprised me?

**The trap:** "Everything went as expected."

**The aim:** the unexpected. The user almost always has surprises but suppresses them because they don't fit the narrative.

Push for:
- "Nothing surprising" → "Even something small? A reaction, a moment, a piece of feedback?"
- Surface-level surprise → "What did that surprise tell you?"

Surprises are signal that the user's model of the world is updating. The signal is in the gap between expectation and reality.

### Q4: What did I learn about my capabilities or limits?

**The trap:** "I'm just trying my best."

**The aim:** concrete updates to self-understanding.

Push for:
- "I learned a lot" → "What specifically?"
- Capability claims → "What did this quarter prove you can do that you weren't sure about?"
- Limits → "What did this quarter prove is harder for you than you thought?"

This question often produces the most useful long-term insights — identity-level updates the user can carry forward.

### Q5: What was my best decision this quarter? My worst?

**The trap:** judging by outcome rather than decision quality.

**The aim:** decision quality, separate from outcome (the decision-journal frame).

If the user has a decision journal:
- Pull the entries reviewed this quarter
- Frame: "Per your decision journal, you had X decisions reviewed. Reading through, which one was the best CALL, separate from the outcome?"

If not:
- Push for the decision lens: "Setting aside how things turned out — what was the best CHOICE you made? Even if it went poorly. What was the worst, even if it went well?"

### Q6: Which relationships strengthened? Which drifted? Honestly?

**The trap:** "Everyone's great."

**The aim:** honest acknowledgment of relationship changes.

Push for:
- Vague answers → "Specific people?"
- Only positives → "Anyone you've drifted from this quarter? That's not necessarily bad — just worth naming."
- Only negatives → "Any unexpected connection that grew?"

If CRM is available, the look-back will have surfaced specific cadence changes. Use those as anchors.

### Q7: What was the most wasted time this quarter?

**The trap:** self-flagellation.

**The aim:** pattern detection. The "wasted time" answer is gold for next quarter's calendar.

Push for:
- "I waste too much time on social media" → too generic. "What pattern of time use, specifically, looks like waste in retrospect?"
- Meeting-related → "Which kind of meeting, or which recurring series?"
- Project-related → "Which project absorbed time disproportionate to outcome?"

This question feeds directly into Session 4's operationalization — what to cancel, batch, or restructure.

## What NOT to do in reflection

### Don't moralize

The skill is not the user's parent or therapist. It surfaces honest reflection without judgment. "You spent 40 hours on a low-priority project — what does that tell you?" is fair. "You really should be better at prioritizing" is not.

### Don't extrapolate

The user reflects; the skill captures. The skill doesn't add interpretation the user didn't make. If the user says "I worked too much," the skill writes "I worked too much" — not "the user is showing signs of burnout."

### Don't rush

Reflection has rhythm. After a user gives a short answer, sometimes the right move is silence — let them think more. "Anything else?" is fine. Constant prompting collapses thoughtful answers into rote ones.

### Don't compare

The skill never says "you reflected better last quarter" or "this is a thin reflection compared to others." Each quarter stands alone.

### Don't generalize across users

This is a personal skill being run for one buyer. The skill doesn't say "most people..." or "research shows..." in reflection. The reflection is about THIS person, this quarter.

## When the user goes shallow

If the user is giving thin answers ("it was fine," "nothing to say"), don't push 5 follow-ups. Try once with a more specific frame, then accept the thin answer.

> "OK — that's what you have today. Some quarters are like that. Moving on."

A thin reflection still belongs in the review file. It's data — the user wasn't in a place to reflect deeply. That's information for the next quarter (maybe they need a different rhythm, or were too depleted).

## When reflection surfaces something heavy

Sometimes reflection touches real things — health concerns, relationship problems, identity questions. The skill is not equipped to address these. If something heavy surfaces:

- Capture what the user said
- Don't try to fix or interpret
- Briefly acknowledge: "That sounds like more than a quarter's worth — worth talking to someone you trust"
- Move on without pressing

Reflection is the skill's job; healing/processing is not.
